# How Forward Deployed Engineering is done at Kepler — Vinoo Ganesh

[![How Forward Deployed Engineering is done at Kepler — Vinoo Ganesh](https://i.ytimg.com/vi/1OMHGsUZiqA/hqdefault.jpg)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1OMHGsUZiqA)

## TL;DR
Vinoo Ganesh explains how Forward Deployed Engineering (FDE) at Palantir originated as a product strategy—not a go-to-market function—using real on-site customer engagements to discover, define, and generalize product solutions that ultimately became Foundry. He shares four hard-won lessons from the field and describes how Kepler is applying the same philosophy today.

## Key Takeaways
- FDE is a **product strategy**, not a role or a sales function; its purpose is to discover what to build by being embedded with customers.
- Customers describe **solutions, not problems**—the FDE's job is to uncover the real underlying problem.
- If a fix takes under a day, **just build and ship it** to close the loop and earn trust; don't over-engineer or involve product management unnecessarily.
- **Physical presence matters**: the most valuable intelligence comes from being on-site, watching users work, not from surveys or conference rooms.
- Look for **repetitive behaviors**—copy-paste between tools, tab-switching, phone-during-workflow—as signals of pain and opportunity.
- Defining the **ontology** (nouns and verbs) of an enterprise locks you in; whoever controls the vocabulary controls the platform narrative.
- Every hack goes to production; ship like it will run for 18 months because it probably will.
- The FDE function should feed insights back into the **core product**, creating leverage across all customers rather than one-off customer success.
- "This is just temporary" is the most dangerous phrase in engineering.
- Product leverage—not customer goodwill alone—is what wins and retains customers.

## Detailed Breakdown

### FDE as Product Strategy, Not Go-To-Market [00:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1OMHGsUZiqA&t=1s)
Ganesh opens by distinguishing how FDE is commonly understood today versus its origins. While FDE later became a go-to-market strategy at Palantir, it began as a **product strategy** in 2013—a way to discover what to build by embedding engineers directly with customers in the most critical environments, including Iraq and Afghanistan. He introduces "Project Frontline," a rotation program that trained ~350 software engineers to become forward-deployed engineers, many of whom now populate companies like OpenAI, Anthropic, and xAI.

### The Phoenix Failure: Building in Isolation [02:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1OMHGsUZiqA&t=155s)
Ganesh recounts building "Phoenix," a data storage product designed entirely in isolation without customer input. When deployed against real financial data, blank values defaulted to the epoch (January 1, 1970), causing Cassandra to attempt creating 2.3 million key-space buckets at startup—requiring 14 terabytes of RAM. The system was dead on arrival. The lesson: the gap wasn't missing information; it was the lack of **co-building while embedded with a customer**.

### Story 1 — Detect the Real Problem and Ship the Real Thing [05:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1OMHGsUZiqA&t=309s)
A large dispatching company submitted a 47-page requirements document for a custom dashboard with 14 metrics and drill-down alerts—an estimated three-month dev project. After four months of scoping, an engineer visited on-site and asked the dispatcher what they actually do Monday morning. The answer: "I check if trucks are late and call the dispatcher." The entire need was solved with a trivial Slack alert in four hours. Ganesh's framework: understand (1) what you're trying to accomplish, (2) what happens after the solution, and (3) how the customer solves it today. If the fix is under a day's work, ship it and close the loop. Whoever **defines the problem owns the solution**—this is why FDEs become valuable in early sales.

### Story 2 — Actions Speak Louder Than Words [08:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1OMHGsUZiqA&t=496s)
A data quality engineer resisted migrating from CSV to Parquet for over a year, insisting "Parquet is way worse." When Ganesh's team visited on-site, they discovered she was manually downloading CSVs from S3 and double-clicking them on her Windows machine for spot checks—something impossible with Parquet files. That night, they built a Parquet viewer. She approved the migration within two days, and pipeline execution time dropped from 17 hours to two. The takeaway: **go on-site**, watch for repetitive tasks, tool-switching, copy-paste between tools, and phone-during-workflow behaviors. "Residents get the truth."

### Story 3 — Define the Language, Control the Narrative [12:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1OMHGsUZiqA&t=743s)
Different teams within an enterprise use different terms for the same entity: Sales calls them "customers," Ops calls them "clients," Finance calls them "billing entities," Devs call them "org IDs." This causes integration failures and data quality issues. Palantir stumbled into the concept of an **ontology**—canonicalizing nouns (entities) and verbs (operations)—which became foundational to Foundry. When users adopt your product, they adopt your language. Terms like "skills" and "MCPs" are modern examples. By defining the vocabulary and codifying it in the platform, you become the **linguistic foundation** that every subsequent solution is built upon.

### Story 4 — Ship Fast but Build for Production [16:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1OMHGsUZiqA&t=990s)
Ganesh wrote a quick Groovy script as a "temporary" data retention fix. Twelve months later, it was running across a nearly 100,000-person customer. His nickname at Palantir became "venue.groovy"—colleagues even showed up at his wedding wearing shirts with that name. The lesson: **every hack goes to production**. If it solves a problem, it will live forever. FDEs must calibrate what they ship by asking: Will I get a 2 a.m. call about this in six months? What's the trade-off for the product as a whole? Who do I hand this off to? "This is just temporary" is the most dangerous phrase in engineering—ship everything like it will run for 18 months.

### The FDE Cheat Sheet and Closing [20:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1OMHGsUZiqA&t=1208s)
Ganesh contrasts the wrong approach—treating FDE as product management plus customer success operational tasks—with the right approach: redefining problems on-site, shipping fixes to win goodwill while owning them end-to-end in the product ecosystem, translating field problems into nouns and verbs that define the platform foundation, and being the one who gets the call when things break. The ultimate goal is **product leverage**—the only thing that wins customers, whether backing a Citadel portfolio manager trading hundreds of millions or a warfighter decision at Palantir. He urges early-stage companies not to treat FDEs as go-to-market extensions but as extensions of the product function.
