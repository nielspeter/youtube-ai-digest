## TL;DR
Rayan Garg and a co-presenter from Theta Software argue that as AI agents take on longer-horizon tasks, the environments used to train and evaluate them must be fundamentally rethought. They unpack how to define "long horizon," why environment complexity and ambiguity matter, how to design robust verifiers (judge agents) for soft-domain tasks, and why many current finance benchmarks are flawed and saturated.

## Key Takeaways
- "Long horizon" is a scalar, moving target: what qualifies as long horizon shifts as model capabilities advance, making binary categorization difficult.
- Two main lenses for measuring horizon are human-equivalent time (e.g., METR's 50% success threshold at N hours) and model-native units (tokens, steps, tool calls), each with strengths and weaknesses.
- Human-time benchmarks break down at the frontier because tasks tedious for humans may be trivial for models, and expert quality varies wildly.
- Environment complexity should be measured along tool coordination (how many tools and dependencies the agent must coordinate) and state-change dynamics (whether early decisions cascade into later ones).
- Sequential complexity—where a misread early step corrupts downstream actions—is far more challenging than parallelizable complexity.
- Ambiguity in starting instructions and artifacts mirrors real human work and tests exploration, but makes standardized evaluation harder.
- For software-style domains without deterministic verifiers, judge/critic models are essential; they evaluate both final state and trajectory.
- Judges are themselves agents: they need environment access (with read-only safeguards), queryable trajectory data, and sub-agent processing for long trajectories.
- Over-constraining rubrics to a single reference solution collapses the solution space; robust rubrics must allow multiple valid paths.
- Existing finance benchmarks (GDP-Val, ToolBench, Apex Agents) are flawed: tasks fall below true long-horizon thresholds, are already saturated, lack domain breadth, and provide insufficiently granular reward signals.

The speakers introduce themselves as co-founders of Theta Software. Rayan is CEO and was previously a founding engineer at DeepSeek working on ternary models; his co-founder is CTO. They frame the talk around agent environments for long-horizon work.

### Defining Long Horizon [00:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2aS7aKoXn64&t=43s)
The horizon at which AI agents can operate autonomously is accelerating. The speakers cite METR as the most prominent benchmark for time horizons, which uses a "human horizon" concept: a model reaching a 16-hour threshold means it can complete tasks that take humans 16 hours with a 50% success rate. However, long horizon is really a scalar metric, not a binary category—what was long horizon a year ago isn't today, and today's definition will shift again.

### Human-Horizon vs. Model-Native Metrics [01:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2aS7aKoXn64&t=104s)
Two approaches exist for defining long horizon. The human-reference approach (METR) benchmarks against human completion time. The model-reference approach uses tokens, steps, and tool calls. Model-native metrics are noisy because they vary by model (e.g., Codex vs. Claude) and harness, but they're still useful for understanding technical frontiers like context-window compaction and trajectory coherence. The speakers argue both lenses should be used together since neither paints the full picture.

### Weaknesses of Human-Based Measurement [04:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2aS7aKoXn64&t=257s)
Human-time benchmarks have blind spots. Tasks tedious for humans (e.g., reformatting a massive Excel file) may be trivial for a model that can write a Python script. Methodology also skews comparisons: expert quality, task definitions, and measurement protocols vary across benchmark creators, making cross-benchmark comparison unreliable—especially at the frontier where only top-percentile humans can do the work.

### Environment Complexity: Tool Coordination [06:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2aS7aKoXn64&t=410s)
As tasks grow longer-horizon, agents must coordinate many tools and external dependencies—Grafana for observability, GitHub for CI/CD, AWS CloudWatch, databases, etc. Environment complexity is a key proxy for model capability: the more tools an agent must move information across, the harder the task.

### Environment Complexity: State Changes [07:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2aS7aKoXn64&t=472s)
Not all long-horizon tasks are equal. Artificially chaining unrelated tasks doesn't meaningfully test capability. True complexity requires that earlier decisions influence later ones. Parallelizable complexity (e.g., spawning sub-agents to analyze different files in parallel) is easier than sequential complexity, where a bad early query or misread log cascades into downstream failures.

### Ambiguity in Environments [09:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2aS7aKoXn64&t=565s)
Real human work involves incomplete information and ambiguity in starting artifacts and instructions. To meaningfully measure model capability, environments should test the agent's ability to explore. The trade-off: ambiguity creates many possible correct paths, making standardized evaluation much harder.

### Verifiers and Judge Models [10:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2aS7aKoXn64&t=626s)
Early RL gains came in hard-verifiable domains (math, data-structure coding). Now the focus is on economically valuable software domains where deterministic verifiers (running tests, writing proofs) are impractical or impossible. Judge/critic models fill this gap by evaluating both the final environment state and the trajectory the agent took. They're also critical for catching reward hacking—e.g., agents escaping sandboxes or peeking at hidden test suites.

### Designing Good Judges: Judges Are Agents [13:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2aS7aKoXn64&t=811s)
Judges need the same environment access as the agent under test. In a deployment-debugging task, the judge must independently check GitHub logs, AWS CloudWatch, and post-deployment status—not just trust the agent's tool calls. Safeguards like read-only permissions prevent the judge from accidentally mutating the environment. As trajectories grow longer and more complex, judges can't simply stuff everything into a context window; they need queryable trajectory databases, sub-agent enrichment, and phase parsing (e.g., separating log-review, code-writing, and verification phases).

### Learnability and Rubric Patterns [16:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2aS7aKoXn64&t=998s)
Reward-signal density from rubrics is crucial for learnability, but overloading density on frontier problems where models struggle can cause judges to apply rubrics inconsistently—requiring extensive QA. Emerging patterns include combining deterministic verifiers with judges (generating artifacts for the judge to review) and dynamic evaluation-time rubrics that give partial credit by assuming earlier steps were correct even if they weren't (like grading a multi-part exam question).

### QA for Rubrics [17:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2aS7aKoXn64&t=1060s)
Each rubric undergoes multiple tests: gold (correct solution passes), no-op (trivial solution fails), and variance tests. As AI becomes involved in rubric creation and expert assistance, additional tests around coverage and expert agreement become essential, especially for long-horizon tasks.

### Flaws in Existing Finance Benchmarks [18:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2aS7aKoXn64&t=1123s)
The speakers critique three finance benchmarks—GDP-Val, ToolBench, and Apex Agents. First, average human hours per task fall below METR's long-horizon threshold for frontier models. Second, these benchmarks are already saturated (e.g., Apex Agents' IB section shows 100% pass@1 on 57% of cases). Third, they lack breadth—GDP-Val covers a narrow set of Excel tasks, Apex Agents focuses on IB, leaving credit, debt, and risk uncovered. Fourth, their reward signals lack the granularity needed for effective training.

### Theta's Finance Data and Closing [20:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2aS7aKoXn64&t=1218s)
Theta's own finance data shows an average of 15 human hours per task across a 50-task sample set. Models take significant time to work through these tasks and still struggle substantially across all finance domains, as reflected in their mean-5 scores—markedly different from the saturated results on existing benchmarks.
