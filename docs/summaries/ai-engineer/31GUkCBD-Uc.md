## TL;DR
Soumya Gupta and Jai Chopra from Uber's computer vision team present their production system for enhancing merchant food photography on Uber Eats at scale. They detail a multimodal agent pipeline—spanning image understanding, routing, iterative editing, and QA—and explain how closed-loop, config-driven auto-tuning keeps every agent aligned with human labels and live production feedback without requiring human intervention.

## Key Takeaways
- Uber Eats is a massive marketplace (~90B annual run rate, 10,000 cities), and photo quality directly impacts user engagement and conversion.
- Smaller merchants often lack the time, know-how, and budget for professional photography, motivating an automated image-enhancement agent.
- The system must thread a needle: improve quality selectively while preserving authenticity, merchant brand, and marketplace diversity—avoiding an "AI look."
- The agent pipeline includes image understanding/routing, an iterative image-editing loop with QA feedback, and a final publish-ready QA gate.
- Flat, unified logging of all agent stages is foundational—enabling diagnosis, aggregation, and self-learning loops from day one.
- Routing is evaluated like a classifier (precision/recall, confusion matrices), with recall as the key guardrail metric to prevent bad images from slipping through.
- A closed-loop auto-tuning system samples production data, compares agent output to human labels, and uses a diagnosis agent + prompt optimizer (reflect + synthesize sub-agents) to update configs automatically.
- Image enhancement uses a "pass at K" metric, measuring whether the QA gate is passed within K iterations of feedback-driven re-editing.
- Pairwise comparison (input vs. output image) is the core eval for generation quality, incorporating faithfulness, completeness, naturalness, and realism.
- Multiple feedback loops—offline golden datasets, internal dogfooding, and production marketplace metrics (e.g., conversion by geo/device/dish)—feed into a generalized diagnoser that routes fixes to the appropriate agent.

## Detailed Breakdown

### Introduction and Uber Eats Scale [00:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=31GUkCBD-Uc&t=1s)
Jai Chopra and Soumya Gupta introduce themselves as part of Uber's computer vision team. They frame the talk around a real production use case: the Uber Eats delivery marketplace, which does roughly 90 billion in annual run rate, adds millions of items monthly, grows 20% year-over-year, and operates in 10,000 cities globally—comparable in scale to Uber's mobility business.

### The Role of Visual Content and the Merchant Problem [01:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=31GUkCBD-Uc&t=95s)
Photos are often the first signal a customer gets about a merchant, making them critical for click-through and conversion. However, smaller independent merchants often lack time, expertise, and budget for quality photography. This problem is compounded when menus are updated over time. The challenge is to help these merchants without making every photo look AI-generated or identical, which would erode consumer trust and collapse marketplace diversity.

### Agent Design Goals and Principles [03:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=31GUkCBD-Uc&t=217s)
The team's goals include preserving authenticity and trust, selectively improving quality, optimizing globally without cannibalizing specific merchants, shipping safely, learning continuously, and operating cost-efficiently at scale. Jai frames agents as sitting on a spectrum between deterministic, brittle rule-based systems and highly creative, unconstrained agents. The design principle is to lean into agent creativity while maintaining safety guardrails.

### End-to-End Agent Orchestration Overview [04:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=31GUkCBD-Uc&t=280s)
Jai walks through a simplified but representative production pipeline. First, an image understanding and routing agent uses multimodality to describe the photo and produce structured output. A router decides whether to enhance or skip (keeping the original). If enhanced, an image-editing agent runs in a loop with feedback from a QA agent. If it fails after multiple loops, the image is not published. A final post-processing and QA step precedes publishing to the menu. Critically, everything is logged in a flat JSON structure, making it accessible to both technical and non-technical team members for diagnosis and aggregation.

### Routing Agent Evaluation [07:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=31GUkCBD-Uc&t=436s)
The router takes the multimodal description, structured output, and metadata, grades against a rubric with pass/fail criteria, and decides whether to enhance or skip. Evaluation resembles a traditional classifier: confusion matrices measure precision and recall. In more sophisticated setups, routing might branch to different models (e.g., a lower-latency, cheaper model), turning the 2×2 matrix into an n×n matrix where each grid indicates correct routing to a specific branch.

### Human Alignment, Golden Datasets, and Offline Tuning [08:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=31GUkCBD-Uc&t=530s)
Soumya explains that human labels are the golden source of truth. The team collects a representative dataset across geographies, dish types, and image qualities, then gives human labelers objective guidelines to minimize subjective bias. The agent's output is compared against this golden dataset; if guardrail metrics (for routing, recall is prioritized to prevent bad images from slipping through) are met, the model ships. Otherwise, tuning continues iteratively.

### Routing Failure Examples [09:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=31GUkCBD-Uc&t=593s)
Soumya shows two failure modes. In one, a high-quality cheeseburger image is incorrectly routed for enhancement, wasting compute and risking degradation. In another, a dish labeled "eight pieces chicken wings" shows only six wings, but the router approves it—risking hallucination of extra wings during enhancement and violating faithfulness.

### Online Drift and Closed-Loop Auto-Tuning [10:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=31GUkCBD-Uc&t=655s)
Static offline models degrade over time as real-world data drifts. The team samples production data at regular intervals, sends it to human labelers with the same guidelines, and compares outputs. Mismatches are fed into a diagnosis agent that localizes the issue and triggers an auto-tuning pipeline. The tuned agent is benchmarked against the golden dataset; if it passes, it ships. This process is fully config-driven and requires no human in the loop, with observability and quick rollback built in.

### Auto-Tuning Deep Dive: Reflect and Synthesize [12:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=31GUkCBD-Uc&t=747s)
Soumya zooms into the auto-tuning mechanism. Unseen eval samples from humans are compared to agent outputs to find mismatches. A prompt optimizer agent—comprising two sub-agents—handles the fix. The "reflect" agent analyzes mismatches, removes noise, and identifies systemic issues. The "synthesize" agent takes this feedback, updates the agent config, and benchmarks again. If the benchmark passes, the new agent version is registered in an agent store and picked up by production runs.

### Image Enhancement Loop and Pass-at-K Metric [13:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=31GUkCBD-Uc&t=808s)
The enhancement process is three steps: generate an image-specific prompt (incorporating description and routing directives), enhance the image, and pass it through a multi-dimensional QA gate (plating, faithfulness, colors, etc.). If QA fails, feedback returns to the prompt generator and the image is re-enhanced. This continues for K iterations; the metric is "pass at K"—the pass rate at the Kth iteration, which should increase with more feedback. An example shows sweet potato fries failing the first iteration (unrealistic plating, wrong portion) but passing the second.

### Pairwise Comparison and Generation Evals [15:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=31GUkCBD-Uc&t=901s)
Jai explains that pass-at-K uses pairwise comparison: assessing whether the output image is better than the input. Defining "better" requires alignment with product, design, policy, and legal teams. Criteria include faithfulness, completeness, naturalness, and realism. The output is a yes/no/unsure verdict.

### Generation Failure Modes [16:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=31GUkCBD-Uc&t=963s)
Jai walks through several failure examples: adding shrimp that wasn't in the original (faithfulness failure), removing sauce from sushi (completeness failure), and an agent oversteering after a creative edit is rejected, falling back to a generic ceramic bowl (reward hacking—a nugatory change with different pixels but no meaningful improvement). Another example shows a plate covering sauce, illustrating how frontier model issues (object coherence, physics plausibility) leak into applied use cases. A final example shows wontons where the count can't be verified, resulting in an "unsure" rejection in production.

### Post-Processing and Publish-Ready QA [17:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=31GUkCBD-Uc&t=1059s)
The final step before publishing is a holistic QA gate that includes policy and quality checks. Jai acknowledges the redundancy with earlier QA but justifies it via the "Swiss cheese model"—layering checks to minimize the chance of failures reaching production. This gate also flags issues that should have been caught upstream.

### Generalized Feedback Loops and the Diagnoser [18:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=31GUkCBD-Uc&t=1119s)
Beyond the model loop (drift alignment with human labels), the team incorporates feedback from internal dogfooding (thumbs up/down, free-form text from merchants, design, and product teams) and production marketplace metrics (e.g., conversion). A higher-level abstraction called the "diagnoser" ingests inputs from all these loops, reflects on which agent needs optimization, and routes fixes to that agent's configuration. Flagged examples (good or bad) are replayed, benchmarked, and pushed as new config versions if they pass.

### Production Metrics and Segment Tuning [20:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=31GUkCBD-Uc&t=1246s)
In production, the team tracks marketplace quality and health metrics, notably conversion (add-to-cart, order completion). Because of the scale of data, they can slice by geography, device type, dish type, and other segments—identifying where improvements land and tuning specifically for underperforming segments.
