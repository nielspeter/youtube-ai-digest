## TL;DR
Kenny Workman, co-founder and CTO of LatchBio, explains how his company is building verifiable benchmarks and agents for AI in biology, starting with spatial biology. By treating biological data analysis like code—where outcomes can be deterministically graded—Latch has created evaluation suites like SpatialBench that frontier labs now use to measure progress, with the goal of making AI agents capable of doing real scientific work.

## Key Takeaways
- Biology is generating data at an explosive rate, with single experiments producing terabytes of information that exceeds what scientists can store on consumer hardware.
- Modern biology research follows a pattern: choose a model, generate data, process it, reason about results, and make a claim—making data analysis a natural executable substrate for training and benchmarking AI.
- Latch transitioned from a data tool vendor to a vertical AI lab focused on benchmark and agent engineering for biology.
- Frontier models lack the capability to extract scientific insight from real-world data, missing the bridge between knowing biology and writing code.
- Latch built SpatialBench, a 146-problem benchmark for spatial biology tasks, borrowing ideas from SWE-bench to create deterministic, function-graded evaluations.
- Good biological benchmarks must be verifiable, durable (invariant across valid analysis paths), and require interaction with data rather than memorized knowledge.
- Human verification revealed that many tasks were ambiguous, with open choices around normalization, thresholds, and definitions that only became apparent when scientists graded each other's work.
- Long-horizon benchmarks simulate entire paper result sections or industry go/no-go drug decisions, but verifiable end-rewards are uninformative, prompting experimentation with rubric-based choke points.
- Latch is expanding beyond spatial biology to single-cell, epigenomics, preclinical pharmacology, and biosecurity, including red-team tasks to evaluate model safety.
- Frontier labs like Anthropic have organically adopted Latch's benchmarks in their model cards, creating a flywheel where competition improves models for Latch's products.

## Detailed Breakdown

### Motivation: Data Explosion in Biology [00:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3ZMUiFaQ3qg&t=12s)
Workman introduces himself as co-founder and CTO of Latch, a vertical AI lab for benchmark and agent engineering. He motivates the talk with a log-linear curve of biological data growth, driven by three experimental classes: single-cell biology (measuring RNA from broken-apart cells), spatial biology (same but with geometric resolution over tissue), and proteomics (measuring proteins). Single-cell experiments yield 2–6 terabytes per run, spatial runs 7 terabytes, and proteomics a few hundred gigabytes—numbers that exceed consumer laptop storage and are growing rapidly.

### Biology Research as an Executable Substrate [02:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3ZMUiFaQ3qg&t=137s)
Modern biology research follows a loose structure: choose a biological model, generate data, process it, reason about results in the context of prior literature, and make a claim. Workman argues that code and data analysis scaffolding provide an executable substrate for biology, analogous to how code provided verifiable substrates for complex software tasks. This introduces a natural way to benchmark and climb AI capability.

### From Data Vendor to Agent Builder [03:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3ZMUiFaQ3qg&t=198s)
Latch started five years ago at Berkeley when Workman was 20, initially as a data tool vendor storing and transforming large experimental data for biotech and pharma. They shifted toward kit manufacturers, white-labeling software for scientists. Over the last two years, agent prototypes started working—using coding models not post-trained on biology tasks—building chat-interface products that dispatch operations to external compute, sometimes taking days or weeks. The agents help scientists iterate on questions like which genes are overexpressed between malignant and non-malignant tissue.

### The Capability Gap and Spatial Biology Focus [05:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3ZMUiFaQ3qg&t=321s)
Workman notes that agentic biology will likely follow the same pattern as coding agents: faulty engineers that improve and eventually work in teams. However, frontier models cannot yet be trusted for real work because they lack the capability to extract scientific insight from real-world data—a skill bridging biology knowledge and code writing. Latch chose spatial biology as a green field with existing customers and a beautiful example of measurement driving progress, requiring deep understanding of how data is captured and analyzed.

### Spatial Biology Capture Technologies [06:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3ZMUiFaQ3qg&t=383s)
Workman presents a tree of spatial biology capture technologies spanning chemistry, optics, semiconductors, and physics. He describes sequencing-based spatial as a specific example: slides with beads of DNA that fuse to RNA in a tissue section, capturing RNA with precise geometric resolution. The output is a large matrix of numbers and a high-content image, processed through highly variable steps across technology types, tissues, and disease contexts, with little field consensus.

### Building SpatialBench [07:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3ZMUiFaQ3qg&t=444s)
Existing benchmarks measured biology in Q&A academic settings, not the experiment-relevant tasks Latch needed. They built SpatialBench in December, with 146 problems spanning different kits and tasks. Because models were bad and end-outcome grading too sparse, they broke tasks into manageable chunks following the analysis DAG. A single evaluation includes one or more data nodes, a task prompt, grader configuration, and a deterministic Python grader—borrowing heavily from SWE-bench.

### Properties of Good Biological Benchmarks [08:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3ZMUiFaQ3qg&t=538s)
Latch identified key properties for good biological tests: verifiability (checkable with a function), durability (reasoning about something invariant across valid analysis paths so correct alternative approaches aren't failed), and requiring data interaction (the conclusion must come from interacting with data, not memorized knowledge). In practice, preventing models from answering in one turn using memorized knowledge proved difficult.

### Human Verification and Problem Ambiguity [09:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3ZMUiFaQ3qg&t=599s)
Science lacks canonical ground truth, so after watching trajectory data from January to March model releases, Latch realized many assumptions were bad. Having scientists grade each other's work became the best proxy. Problem ambiguity was a major issue: tasks contained open choices around splitting gene lists, defining inflammatory genes, normalizing data, selecting appropriate radii, and pulling counts. Many canonical bioinformatics numerical thresholds for QC turned out to be arbitrary, and the rigor of evaluation forced more careful reasoning than doing the work manually.

### Long-Horizon Benchmark Extensions [12:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3ZMUiFaQ3qg&t=724s)
To stay ahead of frontier labs catching up on post-training, Latch built longer, messier tasks simulating entire paper result sections or industry go/no-go drug decisions. These tasks use multiple experiment types, full workflows, and contextualize each step against experimental design and prior literature. Each task took a group of three people a week to make. An example asks whether an agent can reconstruct a metastatic niche—using genetics and mRNA from tumor and metastatic biopsies to find the tumor region that seeded metastatic growth. No models currently solve this.

### Rubrics and Choke Points [13:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3ZMUiFaQ3qg&t=816s)
With long-horizon tasks, verifiable end-rewards are uninformative, so Latch is experimenting with rubrics built from choke points—invariant nodes in the tree of analysis paths. These are associated with verifiable outcomes but only loosely correlated numerically, making them insufficiently reliable for RL or benchmarking. Workman remains confident that verifiability structure will continue to drive intelligence gains.

### Expansion and Frontier Lab Adoption [14:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3ZMUiFaQ3qg&t=846s)
Latch is expanding beyond spatial biology. Their benchmarks now appear in Anthropic's model cards, shown at the Claude science launch, without prior notification. Latch has published benchmarks for single-cell, epigenomics, and long-horizon extensions, and is systematically indexing drug discovery—from discovery to development to translation, stratified by therapeutic and experiment types. They released their first preclinical pharmacology benchmark for small molecules.

### Biosecurity and Red-Team Tasks [15:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3ZMUiFaQ3qg&t=938s)
Latch acquired a biosecurity company and collaborated with American Wetware and Aquid on new work released that morning. Workman notes that current models fail basic biology questions (e.g., about mitochondria) and built routine tasks simulating scientist requests alongside red-team tasks that look innocuous but have sinister structure—like claiming to clone GFP when it's actually a toxin or virus bootstrap. They found routine tasks are used drastically more frequently than red-team tasks, which is concerning.

### Closing and Hiring [17:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3ZMUiFaQ3qg&t=1030s)
Latch operates as a research lab for biology, doing both research and deployment of agents. They maintain customers among kit manufacturers, using that to inform benchmark development. They encourage labs to compete on benchmarks, creating a flywheel that improves models for Latch's products. The company is hiring aggressively across engineering and science.
