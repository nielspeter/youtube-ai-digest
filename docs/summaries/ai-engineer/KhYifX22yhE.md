## TL;DR
Marah Abdin and Robert McHardy from poolside discuss the practical challenges of scaling LLM pre-training, covering synthetic data generation strategies and distributed training infrastructure. They share hard-won lessons from training Laguna M.1, Laguna XS.2, and an upcoming 118B-parameter model, including silent GPU corruption, numerical precision failures, and FP8 race conditions—problems that only surface at scale and require holistic solutions across data, code, and observability.

## Key Takeaways
- Synthetic data is used to complement—not replace—organic data by exposing implicit rationale, planning, and structure that already exists in organic data but isn't optimally presented.
- poolside settled on 13% synthetic data in pre-training for Laguna XS.2, and has since grown a continuously expanding 6-trillion-token synthetic corpus.
- A token-uniqueness problem emerged at scale: high-quality data saturated models early due to non-optimal repetition, prompting rephrasing pipelines to expand diversity.
- Synthetic data pipelines share six modular components—seeds, primary inputs, metadata, secondary inputs, generator function, and supplementary functions (filters/validators)—enabling configurable infrastructure.
- Pipeline complexity ranges from cheap, seed-heavy rephrasing with small models to complex multi-stage workflows for high-value domains like educational data.
- "Hive" is poolside's configurable generation infrastructure: a queue of agents with orchestration and a supervisor layer for dynamic instruction, hierarchy, and policing.
- Distributed training invariants are verified via model replica hashtags—periodic weight hashes across replicas that crash training if they diverge, catching silent corruption.
- A broken GPU caused silent data corruption with no config or data difference between runs—only detectable through replica hash comparison.
- Exploding gradients at ~50K steps were traced to BF16 accumulation before the unembedding head; moving to FP32 accumulation restored convergence.
- FP8 training via DeepChem kernels introduced a race condition silently corrupting ~0.5% of gradients, producing illegal memory accesses and NaNs; a fix is in a public PR.

## Detailed Breakdown

### Introduction and Model Releases [00:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KhYifX22yhE&t=12s)
Marah Abdin introduces poolside, noting their recent shift from enterprise-only models to releasing open-weight models on Hugging Face: Laguna M and Laguna XS. A detailed tech report accompanies the releases. The version numbering (M.1 and XS.2) reflects significant changes between model generations, and the talk focuses on that transition. Robert later previews a soon-to-be-released newer model.

### Why Synthetic Data and the Three Data Initiatives [01:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KhYifX22yhE&t=75s)
Marah outlines three data-side changes to address scale challenges: implementing an "auto mixer" for cheaper sweeps on data clusters before expensive experiments, rethinking web data sampling for higher recall, and increasing reliance on synthetic data in multiple forms. She emphasizes synthetic data is not a replacement for organic data but a complement.

### The Rationale for Synthetic Data [01:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KhYifX22yhE&t=107s)
Organic data contains implicitly hidden features—rationale, planning, structure—that aren't always presented optimally for learning. Synthetic data extracts and projects these features onto new planes, filling gaps and regularizing token presentation. For Laguna XS.2, poolside settled on 13% synthetic data in pre-training (before post-training), and has since grown a 6-trillion-token corpus that continues expanding.

### Token Uniqueness and Rephrasing Pipelines [03:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KhYifX22yhE&t=201s)
Scaling models required scaling the training budget, which exposed a token-uniqueness problem: high-quality data caused non-optimal repetition, saturating models prematurely. The team used rephrasing—a common synthetic data technique—to address this. Ablation results consistently showed improvement when replacing repeated seed tokens with multi-mode rephrased versions. Beyond generic rephrasing, poolside built specialized pipelines for converting between code and text, and another specifically for STEM data.

### Modular Pipeline Architecture [04:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KhYifX22yhE&t=294s)
Every synthetic data pipeline is composed of six components: seeds (primary inputs), metadata, secondary inputs, a generator function (which can be an agent with tools or a prompted model), and supplementary functions like filters and validators. This modularity allows composition of everything from cheap, seed-heavy rephrasing pipelines to expensive, orchestrated workflows. The key principle: if a task is too hard for the model, it loses correctness and diversity—so break it down into simpler sub-tasks.

### Pipeline Shapes and Patterns [06:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KhYifX22yhE&t=389s)
Marah describes four pipeline "shapes": (1) rephrasing/multi-stage rephrasing; (2) multi-stage workflows that aggregate processing incrementally (e.g., generating a novel by first creating setting, characters, plot, then chapters); (3) cross-domain porting (e.g., converting math problems to code, or translating code); and (4) multi-turn roles with iteration, such as two-agent conversations or judge-evolver pipelines running for k rounds.

### Hive: Configurable Generation Infrastructure [08:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KhYifX22yhE&t=481s)
The team built "Hive," an infrastructure for easily constructing synthetic data generations. It features a queue of configurable agents (each with its own prompt, parameters, model, inputs, outputs, entry/exit conditions, and frequency), orchestration between agents for hierarchy and dynamic instruction changes, and a supervisor with a global view that polices the orchestrator. This gives flexibility in which agent runs next, which is skipped, and how instructions adapt.

### Pre-Training and the "Trust Nothing" Philosophy [09:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KhYifX22yhE&t=543s)
Robert McHardy takes over to discuss architecture, distributed training, and numerical issues. He argues for treating data, training code correctness, and implementation holistically—bad data or a bad codebase both prevent good models. His team operates on a "don't trust anything" principle given the many failure modes at scale (billions of parameters, thousands of GPUs).

### Model Replica Hashtags for Invariant Checking [10:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KhYifX22yhE&t=604s)
poolside uses model replica hashtags: periodic weight hashes across distributed data-parallel replicas. Since weights should always be identical across replicas, matching hashes confirm safe continuation; mismatched hashes trigger an immediate crash. This catches silent corruption that would otherwise go undetected.

### Broken GPUs and Silent Data Corruption [10:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KhYifX22yhE&t=634s)
Robert shares a previously undisclosed example: two identical runs (same config, data, implementation) produced dramatically different loss curves and gradient norms. The cause was a single broken GPU introducing silent data corruption—detectable only through the replica hash checks. The spiky loss and huge gradient norms were entirely due to hardware failure.

### Exploding Gradients from BF16 Accumulation [11:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KhYifX22yhE&t=690s)
Around 50,000 steps into Laguna M.1 training, the loss flattened. The root cause: activations grew continuously before the unembedding (LM head), where tensor-parallel accumulation was performed in BF16 by default. At that scale, BF16 lacked sufficient numerical precision, and the error back-propagated through the entire model trunk. Moving the accumulation to FP32 (from a checkpoint) restored convergence and reversed the increasing gradient norm trend.

### From Laguna M.1 to Laguna XS.2 [12:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KhYifX22yhE&t=758s)
All insights—data diversity, reduced repetition, numerical fixes, observability, and architecture optimization—were incorporated into XS.2, making it a new-generation model. XS.2 is open-weight and competitive for its size, particularly for agentic coding. However, at 33B parameters, it's small enough that some scale issues might not surface, motivating further scaling.

### Laguna S: Scaling to 118B Parameters [13:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KhYifX22yhE&t=821s)
Laguna S is a preview model: 118B total parameters, 8B active (MoE), trained on 30 trillion tokens across 4,000 GPUs. This scale was designed to test whether data and architecture improvements held and whether new numerical issues emerged. Something did emerge—unrelated to scale but due to FP8 training.

### FP8 Race Condition in DeepChem Kernels [14:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KhYifX22yhE&t=851s)
FP8 training based on open-source DeepChem kernels caused illegal memory accesses and NaNs in gradients. After debugging, the team traced it to a race condition that silently corrupted approximately 0.5% of the gradient (replaced with random values). This is a blind spot for hash checks because real training has no redundancy to verify forward/backward consistency across replicas. A fix is in a public PR (linked via QR code) but not yet merged into DeepChem. The team is developing a dry-run hash checker to catch such conditions.

### Early Benchmark Results for Laguna S [15:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KhYifX22yhE&t=913s)
Robert shares base-model evals (caveated as partly indicative of final post-trained performance). On coding benchmarks—Multiple Eval Code Bench, Big Code Bench—Laguna S outperforms XS.2, the larger M.1, GLM 4.5 Air, Qwen 3.0 (360), and DeepSeek V4 Flash Max. It's competitive on Big Bench Hard and Eval Plus, and notably strong on SWE-bench Agentless Multilingual (used as a proxy for agentic performance during pre-training). On MMLU Pro (knowledge), it trails Nemotron and DeepSeek—a data gap poolside could plug but deprioritizes because their focus is agentic coding. The key takeaway: the recipe held and scaled.
