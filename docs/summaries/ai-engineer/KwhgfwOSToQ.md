## TL;DR
Kevin Bai, a member of technical staff at Anthropic, explains the origins and mechanics of forward deployed engineering (FDE) as pioneered by Palantir. The talk covers why FDE emerged as a go-to-market strategy for selling technically complex platforms to non-technical buyers, how it differs from a dev shop, and why the rise of agentic AI makes this model increasingly relevant.

## Key Takeaways
- FDE originated at Palantir as a way to sell a deeply technical platform (Foundry) to non-technical enterprise buyers by delivering outcomes rather than software licenses.
- The model only makes sense when you are selling something technically complex to a buyer who lacks the engineering depth to implement it themselves.
- FDE is not a dev shop: engineers must build on top of a shared platform with reusable primitives, never from scratch, or maintenance costs will overwhelm the business.
- Palantir's approach was essentially scaling the concept of a startup "design partnership" into the enterprise — at every deal, not just early-stage product discovery.
- By ACV (average contract value), Palantir leads public SaaS companies in the Fortune 500 at ~$4 million, compared to ServiceNow at $1.2M and Workday at $600K, suggesting the model is commercially effective.
- Before adopting FDE, organizations should ask two questions: do I need to sell something complicated to a non-technical buyer, and do I have (or am I willing to build) a platform with shared primitives?
- The rise of AI and agentic platforms has made nearly every product customizable, which means customers increasingly won't know how to implement solutions on their own — widening the applicability of FDE.
- Bespoke, customer-specific work belongs on the forward-deployed side; anything generalizable should eventually be promoted into the core platform.
- The ideal FDE profile is someone you would hire as a software engineer but who is also trustworthy and effective in front of a customer.

## Detailed Breakdown

### Introduction and Speaker Background [00:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KwhgfwOSToQ&t=16s)
Kevin introduces himself as a member of technical staff at Anthropic on the applied AI team. Previously, he helped build the forward deployed engineering (FDE) function at Rippling, growing it from one person to around 25 in a year, and before that worked at Palantir. He frames the talk as "FDE 101," covering the history of the role, the nature of the function, why Palantir adopted it, and how others might apply it.

### What Palantir and Foundry Do [01:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KwhgfwOSToQ&t=107s)
Palantir builds a software platform called Foundry, which centralizes an organization's data into an ontology — essentially creating "proper nouns" out of data so there is a single source of truth for entities like warehouses. On top of that, Foundry enables companies to build applications. However, selling the technology alone falls short because industry leaders care about business outcomes (e.g., shelf placement, sales throughput), not data organization.

### The Problem with Selling Technology Alone [02:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KwhgfwOSToQ&t=170s)
Customers buying Foundry face a double tax: they pay for the platform and must also train people to use it before realizing value. Palantir realized the solution was to sell both product and service as one combined offering — an outcome. They would send smart engineers to understand the customer's business, build a solution on Foundry, and deliver the finished result.

### The Punnett Square: When FDE Makes Sense [03:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KwhgfwOSToQ&t=232s)
Kevin frames the decision as a two-axis matrix. If you sell a highly technical product (like GitHub or Datadog) to technical buyers (CTOs, software engineers), you don't need FDE. If you sell a configurable but not deeply customizable tool (like Slack, Jira, or Rippling) to a non-technical buyer, that's also fine. FDE is only necessary in the unique quadrant where you must sell something very technical to a non-technical buyer — which was Palantir's situation with Fortune 500 clients in industries like oil and gas.

### Why Palantir Chose FDE for the Enterprise [05:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KwhgfwOSToQ&t=326s)
Large tech companies like Google and Meta have the engineering talent to build their own apps, so Foundry was less interesting to them. But Fortune 500 companies in traditional industries lacked that engineering depth. Rather than trusting them to learn and implement the platform, Palantir loaned them trained engineers who worked closely with the customer — likened to a fine-dining waiter catering to every need — to solve the problem and build the software.

### Commercial Results [06:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KwhgfwOSToQ&t=389s)
Kevin cites ACV (average contract value) among public SaaS companies in the Fortune 500: Palantir is first at roughly $4 million, followed by ServiceNow at $1.2M, Workday at $600K, and no other public SaaS company cracking half a million. He uses this to argue the FDE model is commercially effective, noting Palantir's high valuation at a relatively small headcount.

### FDE as Scaled Design Partnership [07:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KwhgfwOSToQ&t=422s)
Kevin compares FDE to the design partnerships common in early-stage B2B startups, where a company works closely with a customer to figure out what to build. Palantir's core assertion was that this concept didn't have to be limited to a company's early days — it could be scaled into the enterprise as a permanent go-to-market motion.

### The Platform Requirement: FDE vs. Dev Shop [08:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KwhgfwOSToQ&t=484s)
A key risk of scaling custom work is unmaintainable code across many repos. Kevin stresses that if each FDE builds from scratch, you don't have an FDE function — you have a dev shop. What makes FDE different is that engineers build on top of a platform with shared primitives, assembling solutions rather than reinventing the wheel. Without this, maintenance costs will destroy the P&L.

### How to Decide If You Need FDE [09:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KwhgfwOSToQ&t=577s)
Kevin offers two guiding questions for organizations: First, do you have a corner case where you must sell a technically complicated thing to a non-technical buyer? If not, FDE is probably not the right fit — developer relations or a traditional sales-led motion may be better. Second, do you have a platform (or are you willing to invest in building one) with shared primitives? Without that foundation, the maintenance burden will be unsustainable.

### Why AI Makes FDE More Relevant [11:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KwhgfwOSToQ&t=671s)
AI has made it easy to write code and build customizable software, and nearly every platform is becoming agentic. Kevin's hypothesis is that the nature of doing business in software has changed: because platforms are now highly customizable, customers increasingly won't understand how to implement them. Leaving product success in the customer's hands is risky, making FDE more broadly applicable.

### Q&A: Primitives, Collaboration, Platform vs. FDE Split, and Ideal Profile [13:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KwhgfwOSToQ&t=797s)
- **How atomic should primitives be?** It depends on the industry and user base. Some cases allow robust primitives where the app is 60% pre-built; others require granular configurability. AWS is cited as an example of a platform with shared primitives (e.g., DynamoDB) serving a broad customer base.
- **Collaboration between FDEs:** Working with multiple FDEs on a project is encouraged to avoid single points of failure. Having FDEs from different companies work together is akin to a contractor model.
- **What goes on the platform vs. the FDE side?** Bespoke, customer-specific work stays on the forward-deployed side; anything generalizable should eventually be promoted into the core platform. FDE work also serves as a scouting mechanism to discover new product capabilities.
- **Ideal FDE profile:** A customer-facing software engineer — someone you would hire as an engineer but who is also trustworthy in front of a customer.
