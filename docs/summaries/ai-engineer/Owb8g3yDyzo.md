# Why Off-the-Shelf AI Doesn't Understand Money — Udi Menkes, Intuit

[![Why Off-the-Shelf AI Doesn't Understand Money — Udi Menkes, Intuit](https://i.ytimg.com/vi/Owb8g3yDyzo/hqdefault.jpg)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Owb8g3yDyzo)

## TL;DR
Off-the-shelf LLMs produce confident, fluent-sounding financial advice that is often dangerously wrong because they've read about money but never observed real business outcomes. Udi Menkes explains how Intuit closes this gap by grounding AI in millions of verified business trajectories—state, action, and outcome—trained via reinforcement learning to recommend actions that actually work for similar businesses.

## Key Takeaways
- Off-the-shelf LLMs give financial advice based on internet text (blogs, books, advice columns), not on what actually happened to real businesses—producing what Menkes calls the "fluent bluff."
- In Intuit's study of ~100,000 business situations, frontier models advised "acquire new customers" or "increase revenue" over half the time—generic, often impractical recommendations.
- Princeton researchers found that leading LLMs given tools and $1M mostly drove simulated companies bankrupt within 500 days, while a simple rules-based system outperformed nearly all of them.
- Context is not experience: even with all of a company's financial data, a frontier model still lacks the cross-business outcome data that reveals which actions actually work.
- Measuring true causal impact requires comparing similar businesses with different actions and accounting for natural success bias—Intuit uses Conditional Average Treatment Effect (CATE).
- Intuit builds millions of state-action-outcome vectors from real business data across QuickBooks, TurboTax, Credit Karma, and Mailchimp, then trains a reinforcement-learning model to select the best actions.
- A midsize, cheaper model grounded in real outcomes outperformed frontier models on financial advisory quality—the moat is the data, not model access.
- The broader shift is toward "outcome-driven AI"—steering models to achieve verified domain outcomes rather than chasing the biggest model.
- Great advisory experiences need both grounded science and personalization—understanding user preferences and making them feel part of the decision.
- The actionable call: find where your data shows situations across entities with verifiable outcomes, and start grounding your AI there.

## Detailed Breakdown

### The Core Trust Problem with LLM Financial Advice [00:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Owb8g3yDyzo&t=12s)
Menkes opens with an anecdote about his three-year-old daughter's naive theory of money ("Just buy another one" after he scratched the car), drawing a parallel to how LLMs give financial advice that sounds reasonable but lacks real understanding. He asks the audience how many have used LLMs for financial advice—almost everyone raises a hand—but when asked how many trusted and followed the advice, most hands go down. He recounts his own experience feeding a frontier model all his financial context, books, and knowledge for a real estate vs. stock market decision; the model gave a confident recommendation, but flipping one small assumption completely reversed the advice, then reversed again. That moment revealed the advice sounds sound but can't be trusted.

### Real-World Examples of Dangerous LLM Advice [02:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Owb8g3yDyzo&t=171s)
Menkes shares examples from an Intuit study covering ~100,000 business situations. In the first, a new landlord with negative cash flow asks how to improve profit; a frontier model advises acquiring a second rental property—risky for someone already in the red. A model grounded in real outcomes instead recommends raising prices on the existing tenant by 5–10% before renewal, which would actually move the business to profitability. In a second example, an egg supplier with one customer at 70% of revenue and one vendor at nearly all cost asks the same question; the frontier model says raise prices 15–20%, risking the loss of most revenue. The grounded model goes to the cost side and recommends negotiating a 5–10% vendor cost reduction, properly accounting for constraints.

### The "Fluent Bluff" and the Acquire-Customers Pattern [05:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Owb8g3yDyzo&t=326s)
Menkes defines the "fluent bluff" as a generic, confident answer that frontier LLMs produce about money based on what they've read online—not on what actually happened. He previews upcoming Intuit research showing that across ~100,000 businesses, 40% of frontier-model advice boiled down to "acquire a new customer," with another 14% recommending revenue increases—combined, over half the advice was essentially "grow revenue," which is generic and often unactionable.

### Princeton Simulation: LLMs vs. Rules-Based Systems [06:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Owb8g3yDyzo&t=390s)
Menkes cites recent Princeton research asking whether leading models can drive long-horizon business decisions. Models were given a tool harness, data, and $1M to run a 500-day simulation. Most drove the company bankrupt well before 500 days. A simple rules-based system beat almost all the models; even the few models that showed profit in one instance went bankrupt on reruns. This demonstrates that frontier models, despite fluency, lack the experiential grounding to make sound business decisions.

### Context Is Not Experience [09:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Owb8g3yDyzo&t=579s)
Menkes develops the first angle of why models bluff: context ≠ experience. He gives the example of an apparel company where 80% of cost comes from one vendor; the textbook answer is to cut the biggest cost, but that vendor enables 97% of revenue—cutting it yields great margins on zero dollars. He draws an analogy between choosing an adviser: you'd always pick the experienced one over the smart but inexperienced one, and the same should apply to models. Experience, however, is hard to measure.

### Measuring Causal Impact with CATE [11:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Owb8g3yDyzo&t=674s)
Using a restaurant pricing example, Menkes illustrates the challenge of causal inference. A restaurant that raises prices and becomes more profitable might have been naturally successful anyway. Intuit addresses this by comparing two groups of similar businesses with the same propensity to raise prices—one that did, one that didn't. The naive impact calculation ($1,400/day) overstates the effect because it ignores natural success bias; the corrected impact is ~$1,150/day. Intuit measures this through Conditional Average Treatment Effect (CATE), and Menkes urges AI and finance leaders to find where their data reveals cross-entity situations with verifiable outcomes—the one thing off-the-shelf models lack.

### Intuit's Approach: State, Action, Outcome Vectors [13:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Owb8g3yDyzo&t=796s)
Intuit uses frontier models to generate hypothesis candidates for actions, then applies a reinforcement-learning model to determine which actions are actually correct versus mistakes. The process involves creating millions of business trajectories from data across QuickBooks, TurboTax, Credit Karma, and Mailchimp. A "business state" is a detailed summary at a point in time; actions are derived from general ledger entries (e.g., marketing campaigns, payroll, hiring); outcomes are measured across time frames (profit, revenue, cash flow). This produces millions of state-action-outcome vectors used to train an RL model that understands which actions lead to the best outcomes for similar businesses. A third step trains an LLM to generate better advice from this grounding.

### A Cheaper Grounded Model Beats Frontier Models [15:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Owb8g3yDyzo&t=921s)
Intuit tested its grounded model head-to-head against all leading frontier models and found that a midsize, cheaper model outperformed them because of the outcome grounding. Menkes emphasizes that the moat is not model access or size—it's the proprietary data and the ability to embed verified experience. Intuit has built an AI business advisor (currently in beta/research preview) that proactively surfaces opportunities grounded in what similar businesses did and why the recommendation is being made, with drill-down action plans.

### The Era of Outcome-Driven AI [16:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Owb8g3yDyzo&t=985s)
Menkes zooms out: this isn't just about money. We're entering "outcome-driven AI," where the key question shifts from "which model is better" to "how can we steer AI to achieve desired outcomes in our domains." Whether anti-fraud, healthcare, logistics, or developer tools, the winners will be those with the best systems of record who create unique datasets and train models to achieve outcomes. He notes that coding agents already use verified outcomes extensively, but this is largely unexplored in finance and other domains.

### What Makes a Great Advisory Experience [17:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Owb8g3yDyzo&t=1048s)
Beyond the science, Menkes argues a great advisory experience must also understand the user—their preferences, what they like and don't like—and make them feel part of the decision to build trust. Great mentors understand you; great AI advisors should too.

### Three Takeaways and Call to Action [17:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Owb8g3yDyzo&t=1078s)
Menkes closes with three points: every model has read about your domain, but none has watched the plays and their outcomes—and that gap is the whole game; you don't close it with bigger models but by embedding experience through verified outcomes; and off-the-shelf models don't understand money, but grounded in real outcomes, they do. His actionable advice: look in your data for cross-entity situations with verifiable outcomes and start grounding your AI there.
