## TL;DR
Vinoo Ganesh, CEO and co-founder of Kepler, explains how the company built a verifiable AI platform for financial services by pairing non-deterministic LLMs with a deterministic computational substrate. The core insight is that AI has transformed production into a "reading problem"—we can generate vast amounts of content but cannot easily verify it—and Kepler solves this through atomic provenance, scope determinism, and derivation chains that ensure every number is traceable and numerically correct.

## Key Takeaways
- AI has made producing content nearly free, but verifying that content remains an open and critical problem, especially in finance where numerical accuracy is paramount.
- Evals and citations are insufficient for verifiability; a citation is only an after-the-fact audit, not deterministic proof that a number is correct.
- LLMs are probability machines—excellent at reasoning and planning but fundamentally unsuited for deterministic mathematical computation.
- Kepler's platform rests on three pillars: atomic provenance (the model references where a number comes from but never writes or manipulates it), scope determinism (the model decides what to compute but deterministic code does the actual computation), and derivation chains (replayable sequences of steps that produce a given number).
- Verification in finance is not about ground truth—two desks can look at the same data and reach opposite conclusions—it is about respecting the specific rules and logic of each firm.
- The industry's current "token maxing" obsession is misguided; the future is cost optimization and using the right tool for each job, sometimes a single CPU cycle instead of a multi-billion-parameter model.
- The work product itself should be the proof, analogous to how software engineering relies on unit tests, commits, and code reviews stored in perpetuity.
- This approach generalizes beyond finance to legal (case citations), drug discovery (compound identification), and any domain requiring verifiable extraction from complex documents.
- Customers are most excited about reclaiming expensive analyst time from repetitive tasks like parsing earnings transcripts and building initial financial models.

## Detailed Breakdown

### Background and the Verifiability Problem [00:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tt2kX2sgQio&t=1s)
Vinoo Ganesh introduces himself as CEO and co-founder of Kepler, drawing on experience at Palantir (leading compute platform and USG engagements), an alt-data startup exit, and as head of business engineering at Citadel. He frames the talk as a distilled version of an Anthropic case study on Kepler—the only such case study Anthropic has ever produced. His central thesis: AI will inevitably produce powerful work product in financial services, and the industry needs to be ready.

### From Writing Problem to Reading Problem [02:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tt2kX2sgQio&t=125s)
Ganesh observes that every financial services AI talk focuses on producing more—maximizing tokens—while almost none address trustworthiness. He argues that terms like "trust" and "verifiability" have been abused, and that you cannot eval your way from a non-deterministic LLM to deterministic reliability. AI has converted a writing problem into a reading problem: producing content is now trivial, but verifying it is hard. Historically, alpha came from the ability to consume and synthesize information (e.g., Citadel hiring hundreds of analysts), but when a model reads everything, alpha decay is absolute—there is no edge if everyone accesses the same Tegus data.

### Verification vs. Citation [03:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tt2kX2sgQio&t=188s)
Ganesh draws parallels to other high-stakes systems: software has CI/CD and code reviews, medicine has pharmacists double-checking prescriptions, aviation has pilot and co-pilot. Finance, however, relies on an "overworked VP" as its verification layer. He provocatively claims that people buy Bloomberg and FactSet to "displace culpability"—everyone on Wall Street shares the same (potentially incorrect) data. These tools are read-only; the analyst still produces the work product, which is the biggest barrier to meaningful AI adoption. Regulators like the SEC and OCC require justifiable, source-backed trading decisions, making verifiable work product a necessity.

### The Limits of Citations [05:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tt2kX2sgQio&t=313s)
The industry has tried to solve trust through citations—showing where information came from. But Ganesh points out you cannot easily curate those sources; a random Substack post claiming Palantir will hit $3,000 is cited alongside vetted filings. Citations are merely an after-the-fact audit. True verification, by contrast, is a deterministic, repeatable, numerically verifiable mechanism that proves a number is correct—for example, proving a revenue figure was extracted correctly from a 10-K.

### Verification Is Not Ground Truth [06:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tt2kX2sgQio&t=407s)
Verification is not an outcome but a process embedded in the steps required to produce valid information for a specific firm. Finance is unique: two people with identical data can be on opposite sides of a trade. A TMT desk at Citadel might be long a stock while another TMT desk is short, using the same verification mechanisms. Verification means respecting the "nouns and verbs"—the rules—of your organization, not finding universal ground truth.

### AI Needs a Deterministic Substrate [08:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tt2kX2sgQio&t=501s)
Ganesh states bluntly: you do not use AI to produce verifiable work product without augmenting it with a deterministic substrate. AI is great at non-deterministic tasks like figuring out EBITDA adjustments conceptually, but it cannot be responsible for the mathematical adjustments because it is a probability machine optimized for next-token prediction. The model should be treated like a portfolio manager who has access to deterministic tools.

### Atomic Provenance [09:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tt2kX2sgQio&t=563s)
The first of Kepler's three pillars. Kepler trained a model that was 94% accurate at extracting information—impressive, but Ganesh asks who would trade off 94% accuracy. With atomic provenance, the model never writes or manipulates a number; it writes a reference to where the number lives. Databases and deterministic systems handle the actual number. Any number that fails independent verification is stripped out. This is not about having multiple LLMs check each other (probabilistic systems evaluating each other); it is a canonical process of extraction, persistence, and validation.

### Scope Determinism [11:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tt2kX2sgQio&t=718s)
The second pillar, once controversial with VCs but now mainstream. Intelligence is commoditized—GLM-52 is as powerful as Opus 48 and downloadable from Hugging Face. The model is great at reasoning and planning but cannot do math, and there is no reason to run 1+1 through a multi-billion-parameter model instead of one CPU cycle. Kepler splits the deterministic pieces (none of which belong to the model) from the non-deterministic pieces (all of which do). The model decides what to compute; deterministic code does the computing and persists results outside the model's understanding. This is also significantly cheaper.

### Derivation Chains [13:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tt2kX2sgQio&t=839s)
The third pillar addresses ratios and calculations that do not exist directly in filings—gross margin, EBITDA adjustments, enterprise value. Everyone calculates these differently, and what is considered recurring vs. non-recurring varies by firm. Kepler builds a chain of events showing exactly what went into producing a given number, analogous to what an analyst does at a hedge fund, but replayable and rewindable by the model. The system knows which data points it is allowed to produce and which it is never allowed to produce, refusing to extract from raw prose or tables.

### Generalization Beyond Finance [15:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tt2kX2sgQio&t=934s)
Ganesh emphasizes this approach generalizes well past finance. Legal AI tools (like Harvey or Legora) could deterministically extract case entities to prevent hallucinated citations. Drug discovery could process NIH white papers without missing compounds. He compares the current state to "pre-SSL e-commerce"—the TAM is trillions, but no one would put a credit card online before security existed. AI can now produce verifiable work product across industries, which is why foundational labs are pursuing these verticals.

### The Token Maxing Problem and Cost Optimization [17:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tt2kX2sgQio&t=1026s)
Ganesh calls out the absurdity of rewarding employees for "token maxing"—paying a vendor more money and celebrating it. He predicts a rush toward cost optimization, analogous to what happened with Snowflake and Databricks investments years ago. The future is using the right tool for the right job; sometimes one CPU cycle suffices where a multi-billion-parameter model is overkill.

### The Work Product Is the Proof [18:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tt2kX2sgQio&t=1123s)
AI has made production nearly free (and VC-subsidized), but the reading/verification problem remains open. Citations got the industry 50% of the way; the next step is verifiability and provability. In software, every pull request, commit, and code review is stored perpetually—companies are already mining that to build ontologies. Finance needs the same ecosystem. The last remaining mile is personalization: building verifiable ontologies that proxy each firm's unique investment processes.

### Q&A: Provenance Sources and Customer Excitement [20:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tt2kX2sgQio&t=1217s)
In response to a question about where numbers in the provenance ledger come from, Ganesh identifies three sources: extracted information from filings, mathematical calculations (derivative production like ratios), and internal documents combined with external data. Asked what customers are most excited about, he notes that while everyone dreams of an "AI portfolio manager," portfolio managers actually want an "AI analyst." They are most excited about reclaiming expensive analyst time from repetitive tasks—listening to earnings transcripts, opening dozens of 8-Ks and 10-Ks to build the V0 of a financial model—especially given these analysts can earn $600–700K per year.
