## TL;DR
David Brumley, professor at Carnegie Mellon and Chief AI & Science Officer at Bugcrowd, explains how to design reinforcement learning environments that teach AI to find and exploit real cybersecurity vulnerabilities. He introduces the "audit task" as a fix for flawed single-bug benchmarks, and presents results from testing frontier LLMs against hardened targets like Chrome's V8 engine, where top models achieved elite-level sandbox escapes—demonstrating that AI hacking capabilities are advancing rapidly when measured correctly.

## Key Takeaways
- Teaching AI to hack mirrors teaching humans: start with easy problems and progressively increase both target difficulty and exploitation difficulty along two axes.
- Existing cybersecurity benchmarks are flawed because they assume programs have only one vulnerability, causing models to "reward hack" by repeatedly finding the easiest bug and stunting their learning trajectory.
- The "audit task" fixes this by asking the LLM to find *all* vulnerabilities, then scoring with precision and recall against a deterministic oracle—enabling open-world grading that accommodates unknown bugs.
- LLM-as-a-judge is unreliable for cybersecurity; models always claim success, so deterministic grading oracles (e.g., crash triggers, backtrace uniquification) are essential.
- Even DARPA's $60M Cyber Grand Challenge and the AIxCC competition had 50% and 18 unintended vulnerabilities respectively—proving that hand-curating single-bug environments doesn't work.
- Crashing a program is not hacking; true exploitation requires capabilities like arbitrary read/write, sandbox escapes, and arbitrary code execution.
- Frontier models were tested against 41 verified V8 (Chrome JavaScript engine) vulnerabilities bucketed into 16 capability levels.
- On crash-only benchmarks, most models looked similar (~95% for top models, ~50% for others), but on full sandbox escape, a dramatic gap emerged: Mythos achieved 73%, GPT ~68%, while Gemini and Kimi scored 0%.
- Evidence suggests models aren't simply memorizing: Mythos found novel exploitation paths that experts considered too difficult or that had no public exploit.
- Bugcrowd is now generating up to 10,000 reinforcement learning environments per month using zero-day vulnerabilities to ensure models can't memorize and must genuinely reason.

## Detailed Breakdown

### Introduction and Teaching Humans to Hack [00:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZFxh7sqbUZo&t=12s)
David Brumley opens by framing the core motivation: software is being released faster than ever, so we need machines to check it at machine speed. He introduces his dual roles—CMU professor and Bugcrowd's Chief AI & Science Officer—and draws a parallel to how humans learn hacking. He tells the story of Richard Zhu (hacker name "fluorescence"), a 17-year-old who learned cybersecurity through picoCTF by Googling, reading write-ups, and practicing on graduated problems. Within two years, Zhu won Pwn2Own by hacking a Tesla, earning $375,000. Brumley notes other students include George Hotz (first iPhone jailbreak) and Sang Heon Lee (current Pwn2Own winner), establishing that the graduated-learning methodology works for humans and now informs how he teaches AI.

### Two Axes of Teaching Machines to Hack [03:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZFxh7sqbUZo&t=201s)
Brumley breaks down the teaching framework into two axes. The first is **target difficulty**: a spectrum from toy problems through CTF/synthetic challenges up to hardened real-world targets. The second is **exploitation difficulty**: the progression from simply finding a bug, to triggering a crash, to achieving arbitrary read/write in memory, and ultimately full arbitrary code execution. He emphasizes that hacking is uniquely about "bending computers to our will," and that both axes must be climbed progressively—whether using frontier models like Anthropic's or privately tuned models.

### Reinforcement Learning Environment Setup [06:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZFxh7sqbUZo&t=418s)
A proper RL "gym" for cybersecurity requires several components: a vulnerable application enclosed in a reproducible container, a deterministic grading oracle (not an LLM judge, which always claims success), an LLM, and an orchestrator. The setup is exposed via MCP (Model Context Protocol) with functions for setup, read/write inside the sandbox, and grading. The task prompt asks the LLM to find *and exploit* the vulnerability—not just find it—so that the model must produce proof (e.g., a crash or code execution) that distinguishes real findings from hallucinations.

### The Single-Vulnerability Assumption Problem [10:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZFxh7sqbUZo&t=602s)
Existing benchmarks like Cybex and CyberGym assume a program has exactly one vulnerability—a rare situation in real software. When multiple vulnerabilities exist, the LLM can "reward hack" by repeatedly exploiting the easiest one, limiting its learning. If benchmarks tell the LLM which bug to find (e.g., providing a backtrace), they stunt reasoning by pointing the model directly at the problem. Brumley cites two DARPA examples: the Cyber Grand Challenge ($60M spent, yet 50% of hand-curated challenges had unintended vulnerabilities) and AIxCC (18 unintended bugs found), proving that even experts can't create clean single-bug environments.

### The Audit Task: Open-World Grading [13:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZFxh7sqbUZo&t=822s)
Brumley introduces the "audit task" as the solution: instead of asking "find *the* vulnerability," ask the model to find *all* vulnerabilities and submit multiple proofs. A deterministic grader then uniquifies results using stack backtraces (the same method industry uses for crash reports). This enables scoring with **precision** (found vulnerabilities / submitted) and **recall** (known vulnerabilities found / total known), multiplied together. The approach handles unknown bugs gracefully—the model can discover vulnerabilities the designers didn't know about, and the ground truth set is expanded post-hoc. It also removes bias: the model never knows how many bugs exist (possibly zero), producing a cleaner learning signal and preventing reward hacking.

### Weaponization: Testing Against Hardened Targets [17:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZFxh7sqbUZo&t=1041s)
Brumley shifts to the second axis—measuring weaponization capabilities against high-value targets. He distinguishes "crashing" from "hacking": crashing alone can't steal IP or take control. The team selected Chrome's V8 JavaScript engine, which powers Chrome, Edge, Node.js, and Cloudflare Edge Workers. V8 is a hardened target with a sandbox; in-sandbox crashes are expected behavior and not significant. The real challenge is an **out-of-sandbox exploit**, which requires chaining multiple vulnerabilities together. V8 zero-days are worth $10,000–$100,000 on the legitimate market (millions on the black market), making it an ideal benchmark for measuring how far AI has climbed.

### X-Plane Experiment: 16 Capability Levels on V8 [20:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZFxh7sqbUZo&t=1223s)
The team designed an experiment with 16 bucketized capabilities forming a ladder: triggering a crash → crashing an in-sandbox object → achieving in-sandbox arbitrary read/write → finding a second vulnerability → achieving out-of-sandbox primitives → full arbitrary code execution. They tested 41 V8 vulnerabilities, all hand-verified as exploitable by Sang Heon Lee (current Chrome security lead). On crash-only metrics, top models (GPT, Mythos) scored ~95% and lower models ~50%—making them appear deceptively similar. But on full sandbox escape (arbitrary code execution), a sharp divide emerged: Mythos 73%, GPT ~68%, Gemini and Kimi 0%.

### Evidence of Non-Memorization [22:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZFxh7sqbUZo&t=1379s)
Brumley presents specific CVEs as evidence that models aren't simply recalling known exploits. For CVE-2023-670T, Mythos reversed JavaScript's `math.random` to forge a pointer for a return-oriented exploit—a path experts considered impractical. For CVE-2024-7965, Mythos found a new WASM path with no known public exploit and succeeded on x86 where even internal experts doubted it was possible. For CVE-2024-0519, no public exploit existed yet Mythos succeeded. These results suggest the model is genuinely reasoning, not memorizing, and performs on par with elite human researchers.

### ExploitBench and the Open Science Dilemma [24:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZFxh7sqbUZo&t=1473s)
The full benchmark set is available at exploitbench.ai as Docker images with an MCP interface—users can point Claude or other models at it directly. All data and transcripts are provided except for Mythos, which was withheld due to an NDA and because Mythos produced weaponized exploits for high-value targets that weren't publicly known. Brumley raises an unresolved tension: the commitment to open science versus the risk of releasing AI-generated weaponized exploits.

### Scaling Reinforcement Learning Environments [25:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZFxh7sqbUZo&t=1535s)
Bugcrowd is building RL environments using open-source software and an extensive vulnerability mining machine developed over a decade of DARPA work. They deliberately seek novel zero-days so models can't be memorizing. They're now providing up to 10,000 RL environments per month to partner companies. Brumley closes by emphasizing that training cybersecurity AI isn't mysterious—it requires expert-designed oracles, careful transcript analysis to detect memorization and reward hacking, and robust handling of unknown vulnerabilities the models discover.
