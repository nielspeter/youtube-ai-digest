## TL;DR
Maor Bril from Character.ai discusses the challenge of evaluating AI-generated video quality — a problem that has lagged behind rapid advances in video generation models. He describes their journey from slow, expensive human-calibrated LLM-as-a-judge pipelines to a fast, distilled small VLM trained on pairwise comparisons, offering practical lessons on building video eval systems that catch defects early and cheaply.

## Key Takeaways
- Video generation has become remarkably good and nearly free, but evaluation methods remain stuck in the text/image era (CLIP score, LPIPS), which only inspect individual frames and prompt consistency — not storytelling, physics, pacing, or audio sync.
- Existing per-frame metrics miss temporal qualities like whether a character actually walks vs. hovers, whether pacing makes sense, and whether audio is synced to events on screen.
- Using frontier LLMs as judges is slow, prompt-sensitive, and inconsistent; absolute scoring (1–10) is unreliable because raters disagree on what constitutes a "6" vs. an "8."
- Pairwise comparison (A vs. B) produces far more consistent judgments than absolute scoring, and training on pairs generalizes better to detecting relative quality.
- Character.ai distilled a committee of expert metrics + LLM judges into a single small VLM (Qwen) that scores a 15-second video in ~3 seconds, enabling eval inside the generation loop.
- Catching defects early (e.g., character drift between starting frames) is far cheaper than discovering problems after assembling a long-form video from many clips.
- Their first shipped model was "confidently wrong" — it learned to detect the "vibe" (gloss, coherence, AI artifacts) rather than the actual quality axes (storytelling, physics, camera work), because the training data was constructed by corrupting good videos and generating random slop.
- They fixed this by pairing real footage against AI footage with consistent encoding and identical annotation methods, carefully avoiding creating an AI detector.
- The system evolved from a rigid pipeline into an agentic workflow where agents validate their own outputs and adapt to users' distinct stories, characters, and voices.
- Human annotation remains essential: periodic crowd-annotation sessions calibrate the AI judges, and that data feeds the next model version.

## Detailed Breakdown

### The Evaluation Gap in Video Generation [00:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b_PmGocP4rc&t=1s)
Maor introduces himself as a two-year veteran of Character.ai and frames the core problem: while video generation models (Kling, SeaDance, VEO, Sora) have become extraordinarily capable, evaluation has not kept pace. People still "squint" at videos to judge quality. The hard part is no longer making video — it's generating *good enough* video and judging whether it meets the bar.

### Why Per-Frame Metrics Fall Short [02:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b_PmGocP4rc&t=125s)
Existing tools like CLIP score and LPIPS were built for the text and image eras. They excel at checking whether a single frame matches its prompt and detecting drift between frames, but they cannot assess whether the video tells the intended story, whether physics hold (e.g., a character walking downstairs vs. hovering), whether the character remains consistent across shots, whether pacing is believable, and whether audio syncs with visual events. Video is fundamentally a storytelling medium, and these metrics miss that dimension entirely.

### LLM-as-a-Judge and the First Benchmark [04:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b_PmGocP4rc&t=252s)
The next iteration was throwing videos at frontier LLMs, but this approach is slow, highly sensitive to prompt phrasing, and inconsistent across users. Character.ai built a repeatable benchmark harness combining per-frame metrics with a consistent LLM-as-a-judge calibrated by human annotation. Every generated report could be annotated by humans, and that feedback was fed back into the judge's prompt. However, the process was slow and expensive — problematic for a platform where users generate video at scale.

### Bringing Evals Closer to Generation [05:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b_PmGocP4rc&t=348s)
The key insight is that mistakes caught early are far cheaper to fix. If character drift between two starting frames is detected before those frames are used to generate video, correction is inexpensive. Similarly, catching a drifted six-second clip before it is stitched into a multi-minute compilation saves significant cost. The goal is to move evaluation as close to online generation as possible, and earlier in the creative pipeline.

### Distilling the Committee into a Small VLM [07:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b_PmGocP4rc&t=473s)
To solve the speed problem, Character.ai distilled the entire committee of expert metrics and LLM judges into a single small vision-language model (VLM). The model doesn't just return a score — it explains *why* a video is slop (extra limb, physics violation, audio desync). They chose a small Qwen VLM that scores a 15-second video in about 3 seconds. A larger model performed better but was too slow to justify the marginal quality gain.

### Don't Score, Compare [09:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b_PmGocP4rc&t=565s)
A crucial realization: absolute scoring (1–10) is unreliable because different people assign wildly different numbers to the same video. But when shown two videos and asked which tells a better story, most people agree. Training on pairwise comparisons (A vs. B) rather than absolute scores produced far more consistent and generalizable results.

### Manufacturing Badness and the Confidently Wrong V1 [10:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b_PmGocP4rc&t=629s)
Training data was created by corrupting good videos and generating random slop, since high-quality video is abundant online. The first shipped model was "so wrong, but wrong in a very confident way" — it awarded a 9.2 for camera work on a video where the camera never moved, and praised physics on hovering ghosts and flying people. The root cause: the model learned to detect the "vibe" (coherence, gloss, absence of obvious artifacts) rather than the actual quality axes. The fix was to rebuild the dataset.

### Pairing Real vs. AI Footage Without Building an AI Detector [12:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b_PmGocP4rc&t=723s)
To fix the data, Maor paired real human footage against AI-generated footage. The risk was creating an AI detector rather than a quality detector. He mitigated this by ensuring consistent encoding across both sides (no artificial artifacts unique to one set) and using identical annotation methods for all axes on both sets. The result was a model that evaluated genuine quality rather than provenance.

### From Pipeline to Agentic Workflow [13:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b_PmGocP4rc&t=788s)
The system evolved from a rigid pipeline into an agentic workflow. Pipelines work for narrow, unique use cases, but users bring distinct stories, characters, images, and voices that cause drift. Agents equipped with quality-validation tools can adapt to these changes, verify their own work, and self-correct during generation.

### Key Lessons and Closing Advice [14:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b_PmGocP4rc&t=851s)
Maor distills the talk into actionable takeaways: go relative (pairwise), not absolute; score the real axes you care about (storytelling, pacing, physics) rather than expecting them to emerge magically; put eval inside the generation loop as early as possible; and ultimately evaluate video as a story, because video is just another way of telling stories.

### Q&A: Sound Evaluation, Lip Sync, Human Taste, Scale, and Telemetry [15:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b_PmGocP4rc&t=916s)
- **Sound:** Uses Atmos for audio quality and intelligibility; the model identifies key frames from the prompt (e.g., "door slammed") and checks for a sound spike at the corresponding timestamp. It doesn't recognize *what* the sound is — it correlates expected events with audio energy at specific frames.
- **Lip syncing:** Described as "an unsolved problem," especially challenging for animated characters whose mouth movements have no real correlation to speech.
- **Human taste and calibration:** Periodic annotation sessions where everyone spends 10–15 minutes rating videos on random axes; this data calibrates the AI judges and feeds training for the next model version. Taste is subjective, so it's an evolving process.
- **Model choice (Qwen):** Selected for its small size and Character.ai's positive experience with post-training Qwen on other use cases; other models were tried but Qwen was "good enough" and already integrated.
- **Scale:** The distilled model is justified when speed and capacity matter at high volume (thousands or tens of thousands of videos per day). For smaller domains (hundreds of videos), the original committee-of-experts approach with frontier models may be more cost-effective — it's a unit-economics decision.
- **Telemetry/export:** The public repo is a harness that accepts any agents or LLMs; an internal version runs as a service. Maor accepted the feature request to add OpenTelemetry-style trace exports.
