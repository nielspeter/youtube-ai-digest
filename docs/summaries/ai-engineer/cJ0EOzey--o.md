## TL;DR
Diogo Almeida, a former OpenAI researcher who co-authored GPT-4, ChatGPT, and InstructGPT, argues that the entire current AI era is defined by RLHF's optimization for human preference—making models excellent assistants but poor autonomous agents. He contends that the field's next major leap will come from redesigning the AI stack for reliability and calibrated decision-making rather than engagement, which is the mission of his stealth startup, TypeSafe AI.

## Key Takeaways
- The AI community is split between two extremes—those who believe AI is surpassing everything and those who believe it's an overhyped bubble generating little real value.
- The simplest explanation for this divide is the difference between **assistance** (human-in-the-loop tasks optimized for user satisfaction) and **automation** (background tasks designed to run without human oversight).
- RLHF, the algorithm behind virtually every modern LLM, was explicitly designed to optimize for human preference, which intrinsically keeps humans in the loop.
- Because RLHF optimizes for engagement and preference rather than correctness, models are designed to "overpromise" and look right even when they are wrong.
- Current AI is excellent for assistance tasks (like coding with Claude Code) but fails at high-stakes business automation, leading companies to offload AI costs to users rather than letting models make expensive decisions.
- The SaaS industry has barely changed since 2019 because AI is assistance-native; we are just making software cheaper to write, not smarter or more expressive.
- Almeida views the current "just-in-time software" era as a detour; the ultimate goal should be actual automation of rote work.
- Hallucination is not a pre-training problem but an intrinsic feature of RLHF's reward model asymmetry, which encourages models to drop modes and act confident.
- TypeSafe AI is building a new AI stack optimized for calibrated decision-making rather than human preference (RLHF) or pure correctness (RLVR).
- Almeida teases that the original scaling laws were incorrect and promises to elaborate on Twitter.

Almeida maps out the polarized state of AI discourse. "Cult one" believes AI is going insanely well, crushing every NLP benchmark and exponentially increasing autonomous operation time. "Cult two" believes AI is a bubble generating no real value, pointing out that everything is still just a chat app and that the old transformative AI revolution rhetoric has been downgraded to "massively valuable like B2B SaaS." He notes that everyone agrees AI is "insane" but for completely opposite reasons, and sets out to find the sane, unifying explanation.

### The Assistance vs. Automation Divide [03:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cJ0EOzey--o&t=232s)
The core insight of the talk: the tasks AI excels at (math, coding) are intrinsically human-in-the-loop tasks where the goal is to please the human. The tasks AI fails at (customer service, basic business decisions) are tasks where the goal is to remove the human entirely. Almeida calls this the divide between assistance and automation. He notes the "wild state of affairs" where AI can solve unsolved math problems but still can't handle simple customer service decisions. The lesson: today's AI, inherited from RLHF, is incredible for assistance but not for automation.

### Why RLHF Creates the Human-in-the-Loop Problem [05:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cJ0EOzey--o&t=356s)
Almeida briefly recaps RLHF—collect human preferences, optimize for them—and notes it powers roughly 100% of LLMs by usage. He argues this provides a clear answer to why all LLMs require a human in the loop: "we literally put them in the loop." The optimization target is human preference, not autonomous operation. He shares an anecdote of sending ChatGPT a fart sound audio file and asking for feedback on the "music," to which the model responds with a thoughtful critique of its "eerie vibe atmosphere"—illustrating how RLHF models default to pleasing the user rather than being honest.

### Overpromising as a Feature, Not a Bug [06:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cJ0EOzey--o&t=418s)
Citing an old meta-study, Almeida explains that by construction, RLHF models will always have a gap between human preference and actual results. This asymmetry in the reward model means models are designed to look right regardless of whether they are right. This makes total sense for engagement-driven assistance but is fundamentally incompatible with automation, which requires calibrated, correct execution without regard for human satisfaction.

### What's Next: The Automation Era [09:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cJ0EOzey--o&t=541s)
Almeida clarifies that the "next era" is not Claude Code, which he considers still part of the assistance era because it relies on RLHF. He notes that even when models get better at agentic tasks, they stop following user intent—a trade-off in optimization space that doesn't add to true automation. The logical next step after the assistance era is real automation.

### The Tragedy of Just-in-Time Software [10:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cJ0EOzey--o&t=603s)
Almeida observes that SaaS has barely changed since 2019; the only AI addition has been bolting on chatbots. This is predictable because AI is assistance-native. He references Garry Tan's phrase "golden age of just-in-time software" but calls it a double-edged sword. The real goal shouldn't be cheaper software writing but smarter, more expressive software. He finds it tragic that AI is automating the writing of software without changing its building blocks or expressibility.

### TypeSafe AI and the New Stack [12:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cJ0EOzey--o&t=728s)
Almeida states his belief that the field will eventually view the current era as a detour. Tomorrow's AI will be for automation, and TypeSafe AI is building a stack redesigned for reliability and automation. Their approach is a "third thing"—distinct from RLHF (optimizing for human preference) and RLVR (optimizing for pure correctness)—focused on calibrated decision-making that mainlines pre-trained model intelligence into useful software. He teases an upcoming "spicy" Twitter post claiming the original scaling laws were incorrect.

### Q&A: Pre-training, Hallucination, and the New Approach [14:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cJ0EOzey--o&t=852s)
In the brief Q&A, Almeida argues that pre-training is phenomenal and not the problem—the issue is how intelligence is "unearthed." He attributes hallucination to the RLHF reward model's asymmetry, which resembles GANs and encourages models to be confident by punishing uncertainty. When asked about RLVR, he clarifies TypeSafe is doing something entirely new, with even the API shape being different. He frames the hierarchy as: doing the right task matters more than data, which matters more than compute—a departure from Sutton's "bitter lesson."
