## TL;DR
Cormac Brick of Google's AI Edge team makes the case that while small (1–4B parameter) LLMs are increasingly capable and easy to deploy via zero-shot prompting, truly ubiquitous on-device intelligence requires tiny models (50M–500M parameters). By aggressively quantizing and fine-tuning these tiny models with synthetically generated data, developers can deliver fast, offline, privacy-preserving features like voice-to-function calling and dictation to a vastly wider pool of consumer and robotics hardware constrained by DRAM costs.

## Key Takeaways
- **DRAM cost is the primary bottleneck** for edge AI; hardware manufacturers are reducing RAM, and component prices (e.g., Raspberry Pi) have risen significantly, forcing a focus on model quantization and minimal footprints.
- **Small models (1–4B parameters)**, like Gemma 2B, are highly capable and easy to use via zero-shot prompting, but typically require 4+ GB of RAM, restricting them to high-end phones, laptops, and expensive robotics.
- **Tiny models (50M–500M parameters)** are necessary to reach older laptops, low-tier web browsers, and the broader IoT/consumer robotics market, requiring less than 2 GB of RAM.
- **The tiny model playbook is more complex**: while off-the-shelf models exist for ASR, vision, and embeddings, specific agentic tasks often require fine-tuning.
- **Synthetic data is the key to tiny model performance**: generating 10,000 to 10 million synthetic samples to fine-tune a small base model can yield high reliability for fixed tasks, matching or exceeding the quality of much larger models.
- **Voice-to-function calling** is a critical capability for edge/IoT devices with limited UI, and can be built robustly using fine-tuned tiny models (e.g., FunctionGemma).
- **Edge AI offers clear advantages** over cloud inference: low latency, privacy, offline reliability, and significant cost savings at scale.
- **Google's AI Edge team** develops open-source tools (LiteRT, MediaPipe) and optimizes models (like the Gemma family) for edge deployment, often open-sourcing internal first-party solutions.

## Detailed Breakdown

### Introduction and Edge AI Motivation [00:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hacEQHHhu2Q&t=1s)
Cormac Brick introduces the topic of tiny models, contrasting them with the higher-end robots often discussed. For intelligence to reach a massive number of devices, tiny models are essential. He outlines his background as a tech lead on Google's AI Edge team, which develops open-source projects like LiteRT and MediaPipe, delivers edge AI tech to Google products, and works with the Gemma team. The team's playbook is to develop technology for internal use first, then share it via open source. Brick lists the primary reasons for edge AI: latency, privacy, offline reliability, and cost savings (avoiding per-token cloud API fees at scale).

### Edge AI Challenges and DRAM Costs [03:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hacEQHHhu2Q&t=186s)
The main challenge of edge AI is DRAM cost, which Brick describes as a significant constraint that casts a shadow over the field. He notes that mobile phone manufacturers are putting less DRAM in devices, and the cost of a Raspberry Pi with 6GB of RAM has increased by a factor of 2.5x. This reality forces developers to focus heavily on quantization and using the absolute smallest model possible for a task. Other challenges include a wider pool of target devices and the fact that LLM research is heavily skewed toward larger models, leaving the low end less studied.

### Small Models and Capabilities [04:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hacEQHHhu2Q&t=279s)
Brick defines "small" models as typically 1–4 billion parameters. These are often built into OSes (e.g., Android AI Core, Apple Intelligence) and require 4–8 GB of DRAM, restricting them to laptops, high-end phones, and premium electronics. The playbook here is mostly zero-shot prompting and LoRA adapters. He highlights the Gemma 2B (E2B) model, which offers reasoning on par with much larger models from a year ago. Through aggressive quantization (2-bit, 4-bit, 8-bit) and tricks like per-layer embeddings, the model's weight footprint is reduced to 841 MB, requiring about 2 GB of active RAM and 4 GB total system RAM to run.

### Small Model Performance and Demos [07:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hacEQHHhu2Q&t=439s)
Brick reviews decode speeds for the 2B model across devices: 7.6 tokens/sec on a Raspberry Pi (up to 2x faster with MTP), 24 tokens/sec on a Jetson Nano, and nearly 4,000 tokens/sec prefill / 31 tokens/sec decode on a Qualcomm IoT board. This NPU speed allows for near real-time processing of high-resolution image tokens. He mentions the AI Edge Gallery app (available on iOS and Android, fully open source) for testing these models on phones. He also references an open-source Mini v2 robot demo by a DeepMind engineer, showing how a Jetson Nano enables real-time voice and image interaction, while a Raspberry Pi is noticeably slower.

### The Need for Tiny Models [11:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hacEQHHhu2Q&t=689s)
While small models are great if a product can afford the hardware, they are too big for older laptops, consumer edge devices, and scenarios where the model is just a minor background feature. System health also demands smaller models. Brick introduces "tiny" models (50M–500M parameters), which require less than 2 GB of RAM, run on much cheaper hardware, and can be made very fast. The deployment playbook is more complex: off-the-shelf fixed-task models exist (e.g., Apple FastVLM for vision, ASR, and text embeddings), but custom tasks require fine-tuning.

### Fine-Tuning Tiny Models and Synthetic Data [14:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hacEQHHhu2Q&t=875s)
Brick explains the fine-tuning playbook using Google models like Gemma 3 and FunctionGemma (which has extra pre-training for function calling). A 270M parameter model can achieve 45 tokens/sec decode on a Raspberry Pi. He demos a "mobile actions" model that takes arbitrary text and calls 10 different output functions (e.g., scheduling, toggling Wi-Fi) with over 86% reliability. By pairing this with an ASR model, they achieve robust voice-to-function calling—a vital feature for IoT devices with poor UI. The key to this reliability is synthetically generating 10,000 to 10 million data samples for fine-tuning. Google has open-sourced the "mobile actions" dataset on Hugging Face.

### Production Examples and Conclusion [17:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hacEQHHhu2Q&t=1062s)
Brick showcases a production voice dictation app that works offline without a subscription. It uses two fine-tuned tiny Gemma models (in the low hundreds of millions of parameters): one for ASR and one for text polishing (cleaning up "ums" and adding personalization). He also notes that Chrome is shipping developer preview APIs for summarization and proofreading powered by tiny models, allowing features to reach a much wider user base. He concludes that small LLMs are easy and fast on NPUs, but tiny models enable massive device reach, and robust voice-to-function calling is achievable with the right synthetic data investment.

### Q&A and Future Ambitions [20:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hacEQHHhu2Q&t=1226s)
In a brief Q&A, Brick suggests that future ambitions for tiny models include generalizing and simplifying voice-to-function calling, potentially by having an agent automatically generate the required synthetic data. He also sees potential in faster visual models capable of wider tasks like segmentation.
