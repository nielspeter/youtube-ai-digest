## TL;DR

Simon Eskildzen, founder and CEO of Turbopuffer, traces his journey from a self-taught Danish teenager through eight years at Shopify's infrastructure team, to building Turbopuffer—a vector search engine that runs on object storage (S3) instead of expensive DRAM. The conversation covers his "napkin math" approach to systems engineering, how Cursor became his first customer, the CPU-vs-GPU dynamics in the AI infrastructure market, and his refreshingly contrarian views on venture capital and remote work culture.

## Key Takeaways

- **Self-taught origins**: Simon learned programming through PowerPoint, FrontPage, and PHP, eventually competing in the International Olympiad in Informatics, which gave him the confidence to read papers and figure things out from first principles.
- **Shopify as formative**: Eight years at Shopify (starting at age 18) taught him deep infrastructure engineering—from database sharding before Black Friday to building Toxyroxy, a proxy for simulating database failures in CI.
- **Napkin math as a superpower**: Simon maintained a table of hardware performance numbers (DRAM bandwidth, S3 latency, SSD speeds, costs) and used first-principles calculations to challenge benchmarks and infrastructure decisions.
- **Turbopuffer's core insight**: Vector search could be dramatically cheaper by storing data in S3 instead of DRAM, reducing costs from ~$100 per million vectors to $1 per million.
- **Simplicity above all**: The first version of Turbopuffer was an nginx reverse proxy caching S3 objects, running on a single VM—shipped with zero pretense of being a "proper" database company.
- **Cursor as the pivotal first customer**: Cursor's founders had already been thinking about S3-based vector storage; Simon won their trust by showing up in person and helping debug their PostgreSQL issues.
- **CPU scarcity is a real and growing problem**: Reinforcement learning workloads and AI agents are consuming massive CPU resources, creating competition even among large companies for cloud allocations.

## Detailed Breakdown

### Early Computing Journey [00:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jQDXzEVHMSE&t=32s)

Simon's introduction to computers came through an unusual path: PowerPoint animations, Microsoft FrontPage, and eventually HTML and PHP. He describes the progression from making PowerPoint diagrams link to other slides (which he notes becomes Turing complete), to FrontPage (which only worked in Internet Explorer), to discovering raw HTML, and finally to Dreamweaver and PHP. A key bottleneck was hitting the limit of Danish-language programming resources online, which led to a four-year detour into World of Warcraft that dramatically improved his English. He notes that today's LLMs would have eliminated this barrier entirely.

### Competitive Programming and Shopify Recruitment [03:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jQDXzEVHMSE&t=185s)

An internet friend in Australia introduced Simon to the International Olympiad in Informatics (IOI), where he encountered algorithmic problems very different from web development. Meanwhile, Shopify found him through an article he wrote about switching from an iPhone to a Nokia brick phone, which went viral on Hacker News and was featured in the New York Times. A Shopify recruiter connected the dots, and Simon interviewed in Ottawa while still in high school. He deferred to finish high school, then moved to Canada in 2013, initially planning a gap year that turned into eight years.

### Self-Education and Insecurity at Shopify [06:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jQDXzEVHMSE&t=371s)

Simon describes feeling insecure about not having a computer science degree, which drove him to obsessively self-educate. Every time he heard an unfamiliar term at work, he'd note it down and study it that evening. This compulsion to peel back layers—understanding TCP handshakes, TLS, Wireshark traces—naturally pulled him toward infrastructure engineering, where he found himself sitting next to the "people closest to the metal" at lunch. He now looks for this same trait when interviewing engineers.

### Hard Engineering Challenges at Shopify [08:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jQDXzEVHMSE&t=524s)

Shopify was growing 120-140% year-over-year, requiring annual Black Friday capacity planning with physical hardware orders. Simon worked on database sharding (the cutover happened a week before Black Friday), multi-datacenter operations, and resilience engineering. He discovered a mysterious 128GB Redis server no one understood, and built a failure-mode matrix for when various components went down. His most creative solution was Toxyroxy—a layer 4/7 proxy between applications and MySQL that could simulate database failures (slowness, downtime, corruption) in CI, uncovering tens of bugs in MySQL drivers and Rails that were impossible to catch in production.

### Leaving Shopify and the Napkin Math Project [14:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jQDXzEVHMSE&t=899s)

After eight years (2013-2021), Simon wanted novelty. His "napkin math" project—a GitHub table of ~50 hardware performance numbers (DRAM bandwidth, S3 round-trip costs, NVMe speeds, per-gigabyte pricing) with an R script—became his analytical superpower. He used it to challenge poor benchmarking decisions at Shopify, where teams would pick database B over database A based on flawed benchmarks. Simon would calculate the theoretical performance from first principles and compare it to benchmark results, often finding the benchmarks were testing the wrong thing (e.g., distributed queries across 100 nodes inflating P99 latency).

### The Origin of Turbopuffer [20:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jQDXzEVHMSE&t=1238s)

Three factors converged: (1) a frustrating experience with traditional search databases at Shopify, (2) the napkin math framework for understanding what hardware could theoretically achieve, and (3) "angel engineering" work with startups post-Shopify. At Readwise, he built a recommendation engine that worked well but would have cost $30K/month—six times their entire infrastructure budget. The bottleneck was vector storage costs. Simon did napkin math on whether vectors could live in S3 instead of DRAM, spent the summer of 2023 hammering against S3's latency challenges (P99 of ~200ms per round-trip), and finally got a working end-to-end prototype in July 2023.

### First Version and MVP Philosophy [26:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jQDXzEVHMSE&t=1580s)

The initial Turbopuffer was deliberately the simplest possible implementation: cluster vectors, store clusters in numbered files, store centroid IDs in a separate file, and search by downloading centroids then the nearest clusters. No caching layer—just an nginx reverse proxy in front of S3. Cache invalidation was done by shelling out to find and removing files from the nginx cache directory. It ran on a single VM. Simon shipped it on Twitter saying "you could do a million vectors for a dollar" (versus ~$100 previously), with the philosophy: "Why can't you work on a database like it's SaaS?" He knew the invariants were solid (no data loss if VMs shut down; all writes committed to object storage) even if the implementation was minimal.

### Cursor as First Customer [28:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jQDXzEVHMSE&t=1734s)

Cursor reached out after Simon's Twitter launch. Their founders had likely already been thinking about S3-based vector storage—Aman (a Cursor co-founder) had tweeted about using S3 for KV caching. Simon flew from Canada to San Francisco, showed up at their office while they were debugging PostgreSQL/Aurora issues, and helped diagnose autovacuum problems. This built trust. Cursor migrated within two weeks, and Turbopuffer reduced their bill by 95%. Simon credits the outcome to both sides: Cursor took an irrational risk betting on a tiny startup, and Simon demonstrated competence through in-person help.

### Meeting Jensen Huang at Nvidia [35:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jQDXzEVHMSE&t=2138s)

At an Nvidia event, Simon opened his presentation by joking that if Turbopuffer failed, they could pivot into vapes (referencing the company name). Jensen Huang shot back: "Judging by your slide, maybe you should." Simon then asked Jensen if he vapes, which became an internal company legend. Despite being told not to mention CPUs, Simon couldn't stop praising AVX-512, SIMD, and the abundance of CPUs—stopping just short of saying he was glad he didn't need GPUs.

### CPU Scarcity and Cloud Dynamics [38:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jQDXzEVHMSE&t=2327s)

GPUs aren't the only scarce resource—CPUs are increasingly contested. Reinforcement learning workloads require massive CPU capacity (teaching models to search, use tools, run bash, etc.), and AI agents run on CPUs for general-purpose tasks. This creates a feedback loop: as models are applied to more domains (CAD, ship building), even more RL environments spin up. Simon describes fighting for CPU and NVMe SSD allocations even against his own customers. Cloud providers' capacity decisions are driven by power availability in specific regions. Turbopuffer mitigates this by running efficiently across many different machine types (SKUs)—favoring GCP C4s, Z4Ds, and ARM C4As—rather than depending on a single instance type.

### Venture Capital Philosophy [43:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jQDXzEVHMSE&t=2609s)

Simon's approach to fundraising was radically pragmatic. Initially, he didn't know if Turbopuffer was a venture-scale opportunity and didn't want to take money he might not be able to return. His first raise was $700K—enough for two engineers for a year, with himself and Justine still unpaid, and a promise to return everything if it didn't achieve product-market fit. He later articulated six reasons to raise capital: (1) R&D, (2) growth, (3) founder ego (which he calls dangerous and dilutive to employees), (4) employee liquidity, (5) strategic partnerships, (6) M&A. Turbopuffer raised for reasons one and four only. Simon became profitable later in the year and continued hiring.

### Remote Culture and "Campfires" [51:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jQDXzEVHMSE&t=3110s)

Turbopuffer is fully remote, which Simon acknowledges is unusual for a database company (he suggests only San Francisco and New York have the density to do in-person database development well). The company gets everyone together twice a year for offsites (e.g., Berlin, Mexico City). The "campfire" concept encourages spontaneous gatherings—when a few people congregate somewhere, it's declared a campfire and everyone is invited. "Turbo credits" reward extracurricular contributions (conference talks, blog posts, working the expo floor) with business class flight upgrades, encouraging travel and team connection. The culture accommodates both homebodies who never fly and frequent travelers.
