## TL;DR
Thais Castello Branco, founder of Taste Labs, outlines the company's mission to eliminate "AI slop" by decomposing subjective domains like design and writing into measurable, verifiable components. She explains that the core challenge is twofold: solving the measurability problem and countering the "collapse to the mean" inherent in LLMs, advocating for a routing logic that matches decomposed problems to the right solutions—whether RL environments, human judgment, or high-quality preference data.

## Key Takeaways
- **Subjective vs. Objective Domains:** AI excels at coding and math because these domains are inherently decomposable, verifiable, and executable; subjective domains like design and writing lack a single "right" answer, making them far harder to train on.
- **Capability Follows Measurability:** If you can solve the measurability problem for a fuzzy domain, you unlock the ability to build real model capability in that area.
- **Collapse to the Mean:** LLMs predict the most likely next outcome, which is optimal for math but produces "slop" in creative domains where greatness often lives at the tails of the distribution, not the average.
- **Brand as a Decomposable Example:** "Is this great design?" is too vague, but "Is this on brand?" is tractable. Brands already codify colors, typography, motion, and texture—these can serve as ground truth for verification.
- **Routing Logic:** Break a fuzzy problem into smaller components, then route each to the best solution: programmatic verification for near-objective elements, human judgment for contextual or preference-driven ones.
- **Task Design Is the Hardest Part:** Turning a fuzzy task into one with clear ground truth—so it can become an RL environment—is where the real difficulty lies.
- **Multi-Preference Reality:** The world is multi-preference; averaging preferences from diverse people without understanding who they are and why they like something produces noisy, collapsed data.
- **Data Quality Over Quantity:** High-signal data from true experts, with precise language and tied-to-code commentary, vastly outperforms large volumes of messy, unintentional data.
- **Intentional Disagreement in QA:** Human QA should screen for fundamentals (e.g., alignment) but recognize that expert disagreement on style or aesthetics is valuable signal, not flawed data.

### Task Design and Routing Logic [05:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lCBf9slCanI&t=358s)
LLM-as-a-judge is insufficient for brand adherence due to reward hacking and hallucination. The hardest part is task design: creating a task where ground truth is clear. Thais describes asking an agent to create a new page adhering to a brand without merely replicating the original. She frames subjective domains as spectrums: near-objective elements (alignment, typography) can be verified programmatically, while higher-order qualities (style, creativity) require different methods. The key is routing each sub-problem to the right solution.

### Collapse to the Mean and Why Slop Happens [07:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lCBf9slCanI&t=450s)
LLMs predict the most likely next token, assuming the most likely outcome is the optimal one. This holds for math but fails in creative domains, where greatness often comes from actively breaking rules and patterns. This "collapse to the mean" produces the repetitive, average quality we experience as slop. Taste Labs works with over a thousand expert designers across mediums and styles to intentionally force distribution away from mode collapse.

### Pulling Problems Toward Verification [09:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lCBf9slCanI&t=574s)
Thais introduces the analogy of "pulling" fuzzy problems toward verification. Brand adherence on its own is hard to judge, but by codifying which pieces matter and making them observable, you shift it into the realm of verification. The more a problem depends on context, time, or preference, the more it requires human judgment rather than deterministic checks.

### Human Taste and Preference Vectors [10:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lCBf9slCanI&t=636s)
Human judgment still outperforms LLM-as-a-judge in subjective domains. Past preference data collection often collapsed to the mean by gathering inputs from many people without understanding their individual tastes. Thais advocates for creating a "preference vector" for each person—understanding who they are, what they like, and why—so training can intentionally preserve pluralism rather than averaging into noise.

### Data Quality: Specificity, Code-Tied Commentary, and Intentional Disagreement [11:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lCBf9slCanI&t=697s)
Thais outlines controllable levers for data quality: strict expert selection, true distribution forcing, and problem decomposition. She highlights specificity as a correlate of high-signal data—experts who use precise language produce richer training signal. She also describes tying expert commentary directly to code components (e.g., linking a visual critique to the exact code block), which dramatically reduces noise. On human QA, she distinguishes between disagreement on fundamentals (a data flaw) and disagreement on style (valuable signal about genuine preference differences).

### Quality Over Quantity and Closing Message [15:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lCBf9slCanI&t=913s)
Thais closes by advocating for quality over quantity in subjective-domain data. High-quality data is expensive and requires deep domain understanding and high-taste experts, but it yields far better results than large volumes of noisy, unintentional data. She emphasizes focusing on the elements of data quality that can be fully measured and controlled on the provider side.
