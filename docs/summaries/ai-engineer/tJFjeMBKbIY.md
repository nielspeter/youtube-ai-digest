## TL;DR
Shawn Chan, a 15-year finance veteran, argues that AI products in finance fail because they're built for impressive demos rather than the grueling scrutiny of real investment committee memos. He identifies six predictable ways trust breaks in AI systems and five concrete fixes, then reveals that the same trust-building skills that make a product survive diligence are exactly what gets investors to write a check.

## Key Takeaways
- **Demos vs. Memos**: A demo makes a room say "oh" for five minutes; a memo must survive an argument where real money is on the line. Most AI products are built for the former, not the latter.
- **Confidence ≠ Correctness**: AI systems have learned to sound sure of themselves, but being confident and being right are two entirely different skills.
- **The $100 billion typo**: A single unchecked wrong fact in a promotional demo wiped roughly $100 billion in market value from a major tech company in one day.
- **Six trust-breaking patterns**: Unequal source trust, inconsistent numbers, hidden contradictions, melted facts and guesses, untraceable claims, and no accountable human.
- **Contradictions are gifts, not bugs**: When sources disagree, the gap is often the most interesting part of the story. AI tends to smooth over conflicts rather than surface them.
- **The click-through is the product**: If you can't prove a claim in 30 seconds by clicking to its exact source, nothing else matters.
- **You cannot outsource accountability to software**: At the bottom of every real decision, a human signs. An architecture without an accountable person is an "excuse generator."
- **The five fixes are plumbing, not intelligence**: Receipts on every claim, separated facts and guesses, automatic number agreement, surfaced contradictions, and a logged human approval gate.
- **Your pitch deck becomes a memo**: After you leave a fundraising meeting, an investor writes an internal memo about you, and every number you said gets checked against your data room.
- **Money follows trust, not intelligence**: The same skills that fix your product are the skills that get investors to write you a check.

## Detailed Breakdown

### The Confession: Trusting AI-Generated Papers [00:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tJFjeMBKbIY&t=1s)
Chan opens with a personal confession: for 15 years, his job has been to sit in a room, receive a paper from a smart, confident person, and decide whether to believe it before his company commits a hundred million dollars. Now, that confident person is sometimes a chatbot—better written, better formatted, never defensive, and very sure of itself. The problem is that being sure and being right are different skills, and AI has learned confidence faster than correctness.

### The Core Argument [01:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tJFjeMBKbIY&t=95s)
Chan distills his entire talk into two sentences: almost every AI finance product is built to impress people for five minutes, and almost none are built to survive a room whose entire job is to not be impressed. The same skills that fix your product are the skills that get investors to write you a check. He frames the talk as preparation for moments when a CEO demands a demo, a customer's compliance department appears, or a fundraising round looms.

### Background and Credentials [02:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tJFjeMBKbIY&t=157s)
Briefly, Chan covers his background: 15 years of cross-border deals across Hong Kong, mainland China, the UK, and the US—mergers, IPOs, and strategic investments in recognizable companies. He has sat through roughly 200 investment committee meetings and read hundreds of pitch decks. The key lesson from those rooms: money doesn't follow intelligence; money follows trust, and trust is fragile—especially when the author has never said "I'm not sure."

### The Demo vs. The Memo [05:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tJFjeMBKbIY&t=346s)
Chan defines two machines people confuse. Machine one is a demo: one clean document in, one fluent answer out, designed to make a room go "oh" for five minutes. Machine two is a memo: hundreds of pages of filings, transcripts, and messy sources that half disagree with each other, whose job is not to impress but to survive an argument. He illustrates the gap by contrasting summarizing an email (a demo) with defending a mortgage application out loud (a memo).

### The Most Expensive Typo in History [07:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tJFjeMBKbIY&t=442s)
In February 2023, a major tech company launched an AI assistant with a promotional demo in which it gave a wrong answer about a space telescope. The market noticed, and the stock dropped roughly 8% in a day—about $100 billion in value erased by one unchecked sentence. Chan's punchline: even the demo failed the memo test. The moment real money watches, every sentence becomes a memo sentence, and there is no safe demo anymore.

### Six Ways Trust Quietly Breaks [08:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tJFjeMBKbIY&t=539s)
Chan introduces six predictable, quiet ways trust breaks inside AI products every day, promising to keep each short and bring receipts.

### Trust Break #1: Unequal Source Trust [09:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tJFjeMBKbIY&t=570s)
Not every source deserves the same trust, but most AI retrieval systems treat them as equivalent. An audit filing is like an accountant speaking under oath; an analyst note is a confident friend at a party; an internal email is something overheard in an elevator. Chan recounts watching an expensive AI tool confidently cite a rough guess from a group chat while the real audited number sat three rows away in the actual filing—the model just preferred the more enthusiastic-sounding version.

### Trust Break #2: Numbers Must Agree [11:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tJFjeMBKbIY&t=696s)
A memo that says 18% revenue growth on page one and 17.4% in a table on page eleven doesn't fail because of the missing 0.6%—it fails because the room wonders what else wasn't checked. Chan references the American real estate company that let an algorithm buy houses at scale; the algorithm was confidently wrong about prices, leading to a roughly half-billion-dollar write-off, the unit being shut down, and a quarter of staff laid off. The model wasn't stupid; it was unsupervised, with no machinery forcing numbers to agree with reality.

### Trust Break #3: Contradictions Are Gifts [13:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tJFjeMBKbIY&t=792s)
A contradiction is not a bug—it's a gift. When a CEO's number on an earnings call differs from the official filing, that gap is the most interesting thing in the story. Real diligence hunts for that gap, but AI is trained to sound smooth and helpful, so it quietly picks the nicer version and moves on. Chan recalls catching a meaningful discrepancy between a CEO's number and the filing purely because one person happened to have both documents open at once—pure luck, which is not a control. The builder's job isn't to resolve the argument but to make sure it happens in front of a human.

### Trust Break #4: Facts and Guesses Must Stay Separate [15:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tJFjeMBKbIY&t=913s)
AI loves melting facts and guesses into one smooth sentence. "The company will likely receive approval next quarter" reads like a fact but is a guess wearing fact-shaped clothing. A committee's job is to agree with guesses while trusting facts; if the system melts them together, the committee can't find the seams. Chan watched "approval expected soon" morph across three drafts into "approval received"—nobody lied, the guess just wore its costume longer each rewrite until nobody remembered it started as a guess. The fix is embarrassingly cheap: label guesses with a tag, a color, anything that survives being copy-pasted into someone else's slides.

### Trust Break #5: Prove It in 30 Seconds [16:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tJFjeMBKbIY&t=1010s)
Chan recounts the famous story of the New York lawyer who filed a legal brief with six AI-invented court cases—beautiful citations, proper formatting, all fabricated. When the lawyer got suspicious and asked the chatbot if the cases were real, it said yes—like asking the watch seller if the watch is real. The judge fined the lawyer. Chan's rule: the click-through is the product; everything else is well-written packaging. If someone points at a sentence and says "show me where this comes from," you either click once to the exact source paragraph or you're the person opening seven browser tabs in a meeting.

### Trust Break #6: Someone Has to Sign [19:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tJFjeMBKbIY&t=1164s)
An airline chatbot told a grieving customer he could book a full-price ticket and claim a bereavement discount afterwards—a policy that didn't exist. The airline's defense was that the chatbot was "a separate legal entity responsible for its own actions"—the corporate version of "my dog ate my homework." The tribunal didn't buy it. Chan's lesson: you can't outsource accountability to your own software. At the bottom of every real decision, a human signs. If your architecture doesn't have a fundable human at the end, you've built an excuse generator, not a product.

### The Five Fixes [21:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tJFjeMBKbIY&t=1260s)
Chan presents five direct cures, each mapping to a trust-breaking story, and notes these are essentially what he demands from vendors before letting their system near a live deal:
1. **Every claim comes with a receipt**—each sentence linked straight to its source paragraph with the source's trust level attached, not a citation tab at the end.
2. **Facts and guesses stay visibly separate**—at a glance, you see what's proven and what's an estimate.
3. **Numbers agree automatically**—the system refuses to ship a memo where figures don't match, no 2 AM human checking required.
4. **Contradictions get surfaced, never smoothed over**—when sources disagree, the system raises its hand instead of picking the nicer answer.
5. **A real human approval gate, logged**—who reviewed, what changed, when they signed; that log is the audit trail.

Chan emphasizes what's not on the list: a smarter model. All five are plumbing and honesty problems. The winners won't win on benchmark points; they'll win because a tired, skeptical finance person can trust the output at 11 PM without opening seven tabs.

### Your Pitch Deck Becomes a Memo [23:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tJFjeMBKbIY&t=1388s)
Chan closes with the fundraising angle: after you leave a pitch meeting, someone like him sits down and writes an internal memo about you. Every number you said out loud gets checked against your data room, which means everything about surviving document diligence applies to you personally. The same trust-building muscle that fixes your product is what gets investors to write the check.
