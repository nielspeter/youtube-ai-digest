# How Forward Deployed Engineering is done at Factory — Eno Reyes

## TL;DR
Eno Reyes, co-founder and CTO of Factory, explains how Factory approaches forward deployed engineering not as professional services, but as a product-feedback loop where deployed engineers embed with enterprise customers to build "software factories"—autonomous, AI-driven pipelines from signal to deployment. He emphasizes that success depends on "agent readiness" (dense deterministic validation loops in a codebase), clear ROI stories, and model-independent infrastructure that customers own and control.

## Key Takeaways
- Factory's deployed engineers are the "tip of the spear" of the product: they gather signal from large enterprise customers and feed it back to rapidly improve the product, rather than doing consulting work on behalf of clients.
- Factory's core concept is the "software factory"—a pipeline that takes signals (bugs, customer requests, executive directives), triages and plans them, converts them into code changes, validates them, and deploys them, ideally with no human interruption in the signal-to-deploy flow.
- The software factory is "built, not bought"—it requires organizational investment, but Factory provides the canonical agent harness ("Droid") as the building blocks.
- Droid is model-independent: customers can use any model, own all traces and data, apply centralized governance, and even air-gap the system for maximum security (joked to be runnable "in a submarine").
- "Agent readiness" is the measure of how many deterministic validation loops (linters, type checkers, tests, security scans) exist in a codebase; more validation loops let agents operate autonomously for longer on harder tasks.
- Factory's "Missions" product is a long-running agent harness that excels at verifiable problems—e.g., autonomously migrating 30–50 million line codebases—because the agent can self-correct against verification signals.
- Humans are not removed from engineering; they shift up an abstraction layer from directly writing software to maintaining and managing systems that build software.
- Factory aims to create "Epcot"-like exemplary codebases of the future that are advanced enough to inspire but not so extreme that they alienate organizations worried about transformation cost.
- Autonomy ratio (ratio of AI actions to human actions before interruption) is a key metric; Factory's own codebase runs at ~15–20% autonomy with an upper-80% autonomy ratio, and some customer codebases are even more autonomous in constrained domains.
- Ideal deployed engineer candidates include former founders, people fluent in both AI and business communication, and systems thinkers who love designing closed-loop systems.

## Detailed Breakdown

### The Evolution of Forward Deployed Engineering [00:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wpOA-UXynoM&t=1s)
Eno opens by situating forward deployed engineering (FDE) as one of the hottest topics in AI, pioneered by Palantir to embed engineers directly into customer organizations. He notes that the modern FDE role has forked into many interpretations—professional services, product transformation, or net-new builds in the customer environment—and that Factory has a distinct philosophy on where FDEs should sit and what they should do.

### Factory's Philosophy: Tip of the Spear, Not Professional Services [02:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wpOA-UXynoM&t=153s)
Eno draws a clear line: Factory does not want deployed engineers doing consulting or migration work on behalf of customers, even using Factory's product. Instead, deployed engineers are the "tip of the spear of the product"—a stream of information from the largest, most critical customers (engineering leadership, on-the-ground engineers, their thought processes) that flows back into the product to rapidly improve it. The goal is for Factory to self-assemble inside customer environments rather than require manual installation at scale.

### The Software Factory Concept [05:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wpOA-UXynoM&t=308s)
Factory's product provides building blocks for a "software factory"—the implicit process every organization runs where signals from the outside world (customer conversations, bug reports, Slack messages, executive directives) flow in, get prioritized and planned, are converted into code changes, pass through validation (review, QA, security, linters), and deploy. Deployed software then generates new signals, creating a feedback loop. Eno argues this loop is poorly instrumented at most companies, and AI can transform each stage to achieve an uninterrupted signal-to-deploy flow.

### Model Independence, Droid, and Security [07:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wpOA-UXynoM&t=435s)
Factory's agent harness, Droid, is model-independent—customers aren't locked into one model provider and own all traces and data that flow through the system. Centralized governance lets enterprises control where information flows, and Droid can be air-gapped for the most secure environments (finance, healthcare, government). One deployed engineer joked that "you could run Droid in a submarine."

### The Deployed Engineer's Role and ROI [08:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wpOA-UXynoM&t=496s)
Deployed engineers must understand the new model of building software, help construct software factories with customer teams, and ensure the product self-assembles into environments with tens of thousands of engineers and codebases. Critically, they must establish a clear ROI story from the start—e.g., AI code review reduces bug rates by 87%, which improves customer satisfaction, which drives revenue. This requires both engineering and business knowledge.

### Agent Readiness and Validation Loops [11:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wpOA-UXynoM&t=683s)
Agent readiness is the measure of how many deterministic validation loops (linters, type checkers, security scans, end-to-end tests) exist in a codebase. More validation loops let agents operate longer on complex tasks without human intervention. About 30–40% of the low-hanging fruit can be auto-fixed by Droid, but the remaining 60% requires workflow changes and sensitivity to how humans currently develop. Eno compares this to how models get dense reward signals during post-training—verification signals keep agents on track over long, goal-directed tasks.

### Missions: Long-Running Agent Harnesses [12:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wpOA-UXynoM&t=775s)
Factory's "Missions" product is an elaborate, long-running harness for difficult, verifiable knowledge-work problems. It runs with almost no human intervention except at the planning stage. Once a bounded task and its verification criteria are defined, the agent pushes a "lever of inference" until the task is complete. Eno cites examples including autonomously migrating 30–50 million line codebases and building equity research models for financial institutions.

### The Epcot Analogy: Exemplary Codebases of the Future [16:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wpOA-UXynoM&t=996s)
Eno uses Walt Disney's original vision for Epcot—a model city meant to inspire the world—as an analogy for Factory's approach. They want to build working examples of the "codebase of the future" that are advanced enough to inspire other teams to adopt similar practices, but not so extreme that they seem like a "theme park" unattainable for the rest of the org. The balance is demonstrating the future is achievable without scaring away organizations concerned about transformation costs.

### Autonomy Ratios and the Human's Evolving Role [18:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wpOA-UXynoM&t=1087s)
Eno shares that Factory's own codebase runs at roughly 15–20% autonomy with an autonomy ratio (AI actions to human actions before interruption) in the upper 80%. Some customer codebases are even more autonomous in constrained domains. Fully autonomous internal tools (like Factory's "Legal Droid") already exist, but hard visual problems (e.g., terminal flickering) remain difficult to verify. Humans shift from directly manipulating software to maintaining systems that build software—an abstraction-level upgrade that many engineers find challenging.

### Who Should Be a Deployed Engineer [19:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wpOA-UXynoM&t=1179s)
Ideal candidates include former founders (each SDLC stage Droid addresses is seen as a potential billion-dollar business), people fluent in AI with strong business acumen and executive presence, and systems thinkers who love designing closed-loop systems and modeling data flow through complex organizations. Eno invites interested people to email him directly or apply to the "Engineer, Deployed" role.
