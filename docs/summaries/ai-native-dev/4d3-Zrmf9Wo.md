## TL;DR
Simon Obstbaum (Stanford's Software Engineering Productivity Research Group) and Rob Willoughby (Tessal) present a dual top-down and bottoms-up view on evaluating AI agent performance. Drawing on a 150,000-engineer productivity study, they show that structured AI tooling—particularly "skills" (L3 artifacts)—dramatically improves code quality and instruction-following without sacrificing throughput, while also revealing major shifts in engineer productivity rankings. They introduce measurement frameworks for skill activation, trajectory, and outcome, and announce the Stanford AI Spend Index to benchmark token spend per developer.

## Key Takeaways
- The software industry is shifting from "vibes-based" AI evaluation to rigorous, metrics-driven assessment of agent performance.
- Stanford's Sweeper Lab study of ~150,000 engineers uses expert panels and ML models to measure output, productivity, and code quality at scale.
- AI adoption is creating a growing performance gap: top performers using AI are pulling far ahead of laggards, with up to 60% output differences observed by mid-2026.
- Productivity rank stability dropped from 0.70 to 0.45 post-AI, meaning the tactics that made someone a top performer before AI no longer guarantee top performance.
- Repos are classified into four levels of AI tooling maturity; Level 1 (no AI config) shows degraded code quality, while Level 2+ shows improvements across PR throughput, revert rate, complexity, and duplication.
- Skills (L3 artifacts) encode organization-specific instructions, conventions, and prohibited patterns that agents can't know from training data alone.
- Goal completion stays at ~90–93% regardless of skill presence, but instruction-following improves significantly when skills are present—55% of instructions are followed even without the skill, meaning the model already knows them.
- The biggest value from skills comes in areas unique to the business: security/compliance, internal conventions, media/file processing, and deprecated pattern avoidance.
- Measurement should span three dimensions: skill activation (did it trigger?), trajectory (did it follow the intended workflow?), and outcome (did it complete the task?).
- Changing the harness around a model can move scores by up to 100%, so the full system—model plus harness—must be evaluated together.
- Stanford launched the AI Spend Index to benchmark monthly AI token spend per developer across participating organizations.

### From Vibes to Metrics: Framing the Problem [02:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4d3-Zrmf9Wo&t=154s)
Rob opens by framing the core problem: the AI development community has historically relied on "vibes-based" assessment—whether demos look cool or agents seem to produce reasonable code. He argues that just as software engineering matured as a discipline, "agentic engineering" must mature toward understanding and optimizing what happens behind the hood. The talk is structured around two perspectives: a top-down correlational view across the industry (Simon) and a bottoms-up view zooming into how a single agent uses one skill for one task (Rob).

### Stanford's Sweeper Lab Productivity Study [04:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4d3-Zrmf9Wo&t=249s)
Simon introduces the Sweeper Lab study—the largest software engineering productivity study to date, with roughly 150,000 enrolled engineers across companies committing to a one-year observation period. The study analyzes entire source code repositories across all languages. Rather than counting commits or lines of code, they use a panel of experts who assess code on time-to-implement, quality, maintainability, and complexity. A surprising finding was high agreement both within and across panels. They built an ML model to replicate the expert panel at scale, validated against time-logging data from participating companies.

### AI's Impact on Team and Individual Performance [07:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4d3-Zrmf9Wo&t=433s)
Simon presents data on 46 teams using AI over an extended period. After a methodology adjustment in July (because control groups disappeared as everyone adopted AI), the data shows the performance gap widening dramatically—from ~5% in Q1 2023 to up to 60% by July 2026 between AI-using and non-AI-using teams. Within AI-adopting teams, the discrepancy is vast: laggards show no meaningful change, while top performers pull far ahead. Simon notes the emergence of what resembles a "10x engineer"—people who know how to orchestrate agents achieve significantly better outcomes.

### Productivity Rank Stability and Role Shifts [10:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4d3-Zrmf9Wo&t=656s)
A striking finding: rank stability among engineer performance quartiles dropped from 0.70 pre-AI to 0.45 post-AI. The tactics that previously made someone a top performer no longer apply. Simon hypothesizes that some former bottom-quartile performers (possibly staff/principal engineers who spent time on code reviews and mentoring) moved to top quartile as AI automated parts of their helper role, freeing them to code directly. Conversely, some previously top performers moved down. The full dynamics are not yet understood—only observed.

### Agent Orchestration Patterns and Repository Levels [12:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4d3-Zrmf9Wo&t=750s)
Simon previews a paper submitted to the IEEE ASE conference examining how people orchestrate agents and what artifacts appear in repositories. Repos are classified into four levels based on AI tooling maturity. Level 1 (no AI config, harnessing, skills, or instructions) shows degraded outcomes: more cognitive complexity, static warnings, code duplication, and limited agent capability. Level 2 shows significant improvements—increased PR throughput, decreased revert rate, reduced complexity and duplication. The earlier argument that AI would increase rework and defects no longer holds for Level 2+ teams.

### Zooming In: Skills as the L3 Artifact [15:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4d3-Zrmf9Wo&t=938s)
Rob takes over to shift from the macro to the micro view. He introduces "skills" as the L3 artifact Simon referenced—the structured context that influences how an agent completes a task. The key insight: the work gets finished either way (commits ship at the same velocity), but what changes is *how* the agent finishes it—specifically, instruction following. Rob's dataset comprises 500 skills, 1,000 tasks, and 19 configs (combinations of models and harnesses). Tasks are synthetically derived but grounded in the skills themselves, scoped like mid-level Jira tickets.

### Goal Completion vs. Instruction Following [17:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4d3-Zrmf9Wo&t=1029s)
Goal completion hits 90–93% regardless of skill presence, because the tasks are intentionally not frontier-pushing. The differentiator is instruction following—rubrics grounded in what the skill tells the agent to do (e.g., use specific libraries, follow internal design patterns, update to certain versions). A notable finding: 55% of instructions are followed even when the skill is absent, meaning that information is already in the model's weights. This raises the question of whether some skills are just burning inference tokens to tell the model what it would do anyway.

### Three Categories Where Skills Drive Improvement [19:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4d3-Zrmf9Wo&t=1152s)
Rob identifies three repeated categories where structured skills change agent behavior: (1) Library and API choices—enforcing internal allowed/prohibited libraries, avoiding fresh NPM packages, etc.; (2) Conventions and required steps—infrastructure setup, credential configuration, deployment sequences unique to the engineering environment; (3) Prohibited or deprecated patterns—e.g., never exposing something to the internet without a security-team-managed reverse proxy. These encode unique business IP that the agent cannot know from training data.

### Concrete Example: Hugging Face CLI [20:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4d3-Zrmf9Wo&t=1246s)
Rob illustrates with a real example: Hugging Face has an old CLI (`huggingface_cli`) and a new CLI (`hf`) released after model training cutoffs. A naive agent will use the old CLI, which still works due to backward compatibility, so task completion passes—but the team is stuck at a point in time, unable to roll forward until the new CLI enters training data (6–9 months out). A skill encoding the new CLI pattern changes this at inference time. Throughput looks identical, but the standard of correctness is higher.

### Where Skills Provide the Most Lift [22:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4d3-Zrmf9Wo&t=1337s)
Using K-nearest-neighbor clustering, Rob's team found the biggest instruction-following lifts in: media/file processing (internal conventions), content/docs (house style), and security/compliance (organization-specific policies). Data processing showed less lift (generic Pandas transforms aren't very differentiable). Testing/QA also showed less lift—small models are extensively trained on TDD, so unique testing styles may not add much value unless meaningfully better. The takeaway: invest in structure where conventions are local and important to your business.

### What to Measure: Activation, Trajectory, Outcome [23:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4d3-Zrmf9Wo&t=1430s)
Rob argues that just measuring the outcome (did it write code, pass tests?) is insufficient. You should measure across three dimensions: (1) Skill activation—did the skill actually trigger? (Claude may ignore skills when 20–30 are in context; descriptions should be optimized, especially if they exceed ~2% of the context budget). (2) Trajectory—did the run follow the intended workflow and steps in sequence? (3) Outcome—the standard completion check. He also notes that changing the harness around a model can move scores by up to 100%, so you must test the full system—model plus harness—together, not just "Opus 4.8" in the abstract.

### Stanford AI Spend Index and Wrap-Up [25:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4d3-Zrmf9Wo&t=1522s)
Simon returns to introduce the Stanford AI Spend Index, publishing monthly AI spend per developer statistics based on study participants. Organizations can submit their own data and unlock more granular benchmarks. The goal is to connect productivity and outcome research with token spend, addressing industry uncertainty about appropriate spend levels. The level analysis is available as open source on the lab's website. Simon and Rob share their respective URLs.

### Q&A: Age, Performance Ratings, Harness Data, Code Quality, and Team Dynamics [27:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4d3-Zrmf9Wo&t=1646s)
- **Age and productivity shifts**: Simon confirms age isn't tracked (regulatory concerns), but title data suggests the Q1-to-top movement is concentrated at staff/principal engineer levels. An audience member observes the conference's senior-skewed demographic.
- **Bad performance ratings for non-AI users**: Simon clarifies their analysis is purely output-based via the ML model, not HR-driven, so it doesn't bias against non-AI users.
- **Harness benchmarking**: Rob acknowledges the gap and says a paper with 19-config permutation data is headed to arXiv, with plans to publish regularly on new model releases.
- **Code quality conventions**: Rob clarifies the study looked at instruction-following, not specific coding standards. Simon notes maintainability (higher expert agreement) is used rather than subjective "quality."
- **Time to high performance and triggers**: Simon says they observe a "mindset switch" but haven't studied triggers or timelines. Top AI performers tend to stay in that bucket, and team dynamics matter—individuals dragged down by non-AI-compatible teams underperform.
