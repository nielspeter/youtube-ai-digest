# Claude Opus 5 one-shotted this game...

[![Claude Opus 5 one-shotted this game...](https://i.ytimg.com/vi/viHqe5QqTd0/hqdefault.jpg)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viHqe5QqTd0)

## TL;DR
The video explores the "Gauntlet Loop" prompt—popularized by Matt Shumer—that instructs Claude Opus 5 to autonomously generate a complete 3D game using sub-agents and a harsh critic loop. The creator spent approximately $1,200 in token usage over 19 hours to generate a Formula 1 game called "Apex GP," yielding an impressive but buggy result entirely from a four-sentence prompt with no external assets.

## Key Takeaways
- The "Gauntlet Loop" prompt gives Claude a goal and a real-world target (e.g., Call of Duty or F1), then uses sub-agents that loop through coding and critic feedback until a quality threshold is met.
- Claude autonomously created its own tooling harness, including screenshot tools, game-state extractors, regression comparison tools, and 136 single-purpose tools (e.g., material A/B testing, scripted driving).
- The creator's run used 1.7 billion tokens across 137 agents over 19 hours, costing roughly $1,200, and stopped at round five with an overall critic score of 67.3 out of a target of 90.
- The resulting game, "Apex GP," features quick race, full race weekend, time trial, and settings modes with selectable drivers, liveries, weather, difficulty, and multiple camera angles.
- Graphics—including the F1 car model, track textures, shadows, sparks, and rain effects—were surprisingly impressive for a fully AI-generated Three.js game with no external assets.
- Gameplay issues include overly sensitive steering, cars clipping through barriers, no proper race finish or podium screen, and penalties that don't seem to affect the outcome.
- The experiment demonstrates a new paradigm: prompting entire software systems into existence rather than hand-coding them, though costs and limitations make it impractical for production use today.

### How the Gauntlet Loop Works [01:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viHqe5QqTd0&t=93s)
The original prompt gives a lead agent two things: a goal and a real-world target (e.g., Call of Duty). It then asks the agent to use sub-agents, each looping over a small piece of the game. Each sub-agent codes, then passes output to a "harsh critic" that examines screenshots and feeds feedback back, looping until the critic is satisfied. The narrator's run looped for 19 hours before being manually stopped. A newer "meta prompt" version exists where you pass instructions to an agent that generates the final prompt for you.

### Claude's Autonomous Workflow [02:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viHqe5QqTd0&t=154s)
For the narrator's Formula 1 request, Claude created its own screenshot tools to capture the game frame-by-frame from multiple camera angles. It then launched a workflow with 30 agents across five phases: one agent for the core engine foundation, 15 agents for systems (environment, models, camera effects), an integration agent, and five "harsh art director" critic agents evaluating silhouettes, materials, lighting, world density, and motion. The critics targeted a score of 90, meaning indistinguishable from the original game.

### Scoring and Self-Built Tooling [03:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viHqe5QqTd0&t=215s)
By round five, critics scored the game across car, world, and motion categories, reaching an overall score of 67.3. Claude itself suggested the goal might be unrealistic and that scores would plateau in the 70s. Along the way, Claude built three permanent tools (screenshot capture, game-state extraction for shadow/lighting/material data, and screenshot regression comparison) plus 136 single-purpose tools including material A/B testing and a scripted driving tool. It used over 2,000 screenshots in total.

### Cost and Scale [04:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viHqe5QqTd0&t=277s)
The run consumed 1.7 billion tokens across 137 agents over 19 hours, reaching approximately $1,200 in usage. The narrator notes this was on a subscription plan, so it was effectively "kind of free."

### The Final Game: Apex GP [05:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viHqe5QqTd0&t=308s)
The generated game, called "Apex GP," features four modes: quick race, full race weekend, time trial, and settings. Settings include camera options, HUD, and difficulty. The narrator selects driver Norris with a heritage livery, notes only one circuit is available, and observes that Claude used real-world driver names and attempted real F1 liveries like Mercedes and Ferrari.

### Graphics and Gameplay Impressions [05:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viHqe5QqTd0&t=339s)
The narrator expresses genuine surprise at the quality of the F1 car model, road textures, crowd details, shadows, and spark effects. A formation lap plays automatically. However, steering is extremely sensitive, cars clip through barriers, and a major crash occurs. Multiple camera views are available—cockpit, halo, TV, and wide angle—though some are undrivable and one has a bug placing the camera inside a building.

### Weather, Penalties, and Race Finish [07:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viHqe5QqTd0&t=465s)
A time trial under heavy rain shows cloudy, gray environments but decent spray effects and puddles. The game implements penalties for jumping the start and track limit violations. However, crossing the finish line produces no podium or ending screen—the race simply continues with extra laps.

### Final Verdict [09:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viHqe5QqTd0&t=556s)
The narrator is "pretty damn impressed" with the results, noting the cars, track, and environment look good for something entirely generated by Opus 5 with no external assets. While not playable beyond a few minutes in its current state, the narrator believes a few more prompts could fix barriers and handling. The code was uploaded to a GitHub repo, and the narrator admits to not having looked at a single line of the generated code—it was purely a fun experiment.
