# What the Best Engineers Do About AI Costs

## TL;DR
Amos, a platform engineering leader at Booking.com, discusses how the AI coding revolution is shifting bottlenecks from writing code to verification and other upstream processes. Drawing on his team's four years of building a massive event-tracking system, he explains how measuring developer experience and output—rather than enforcing rigid metrics or token budgets—allows large enterprises to balance the speed of AI with the reliability and compliance their customers demand.

## Key Takeaways
*   **Bottlenecks are shifting:** AI makes coding faster, but the bottleneck is moving to verification (code review, security, compliance) and upstream processes like design and business heuristics.
*   **Verification is becoming a stack of agents:** Instead of a single pull request review, verification now involves multiple agents checking for architecture guidelines, security vulnerabilities, and regulatory compliance.
*   **Customer trust dictates quality:** Unlike some AI labs that can tolerate lower uptime, e-commerce businesses like Booking.com cannot afford downtime, making code quality and reliability paramount.
*   **Cost is a new bottleneck:** The industry is repeating the cloud migration pattern—initially ignoring costs, only for them to become a major concern. Model routing and flexibility are essential for managing these expenses.
*   **We are in a bubble, but the technology is real:** Comparing AI to the dot-com bubble, Amos notes that while the economic structure is uncertain, the underlying technology will persist and evolve.
*   **Measuring output requires a foundational event store:** Booking.com built a system collecting millions of daily events (commits, CI builds, tickets) to investigate bottlenecks rather than to enforce gamable targets.
*   **Avoid Goodhart's Law:** Static dashboards and rigid metrics (like lines of code or commit counts) are easily gamed; an investigative, query-based approach is more effective.
*   **Developer experience equals velocity:** High trust, qualitative feedback (via surveys), and a seamless environment lead to both happier engineers and higher productivity.
*   **Everyone is becoming a builder:** Non-engineers are now creating software, requiring platform teams to provide guardrails, observability, and maintenance support for these new creators.

## Detailed Breakdown

### The Shifting Bottleneck [00:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U2p01LVC5Oo&t=32s)
Amos explains that while AI accelerates coding, the bottleneck is moving to verification, specifically code reviews. He generalizes this using Andy Grove's "breakfast diner" metaphor from *High Output Management*: no matter how fast you prepare other ingredients, if eggs take ten minutes to boil, breakfast takes ten minutes. Similarly, if design or business heuristics lag, faster coding won't speed up overall delivery.

### Scale and the Verification Problem [03:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U2p01LVC5Oo&t=224s)
Booking.com operates with around 4,000 engineers across 8,000 repositories, ranging from 25-year-old monoliths to new microservices. The rise of "everyone is an engineer" means new, non-traditional code contributors are emerging. Amos highlights that verification harnesses must differ by discipline—for example, mobile engineers need app store verification, while web engineers do not.

### Customer Trust and Uptime [06:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U2p01LVC5Oo&t=419s)
Amos stresses that businesses must stay pragmatic. While some may look at AI labs like OpenAI and their 92% uptime as a model, e-commerce companies require "five nines" (99.999%) availability. Moving fast is important, but breaking production erodes customer trust, which is the core of the business.

### Verification as a Stack of Agents [09:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U2p01LVC5Oo&t=578s)
Verification is no longer just a pull request review; it is becoming a stack of specialized agents. This includes agents for architectural guidelines, security vulnerability detection, and financial or app store regulatory compliance. Running these agents securely and quickly requires robust infrastructure to spin up isolated containers or VMs.

### The Cost Reckoning and the AI Bubble [13:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U2p01LVC5Oo&t=807s)
Just as cloud migration initially ignored costs until provider margins kicked in, AI is entering a cost reckoning. Amos draws parallels to the dot-com bubble: the internet was a bubble, but the technology didn't go away. Similarly, AI's economic structure is uncertain, but the value is real. Companies must stay flexible, as the winning model provider (OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta) or approach (local vs. cloud) is still unknown.

### Historical Parallels: General Magic [21:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U2p01LVC5Oo&t=1289s)
Amos shares the story of General Magic, a 1990s company that tried to build a handheld device (the "Crystal") resembling an iPhone, decades too early. He uses this to illustrate that ideas in tech often arrive before the market or operational capabilities are ready, but the seeds planted by "crazy" early pioneers eventually revolutionize the industry.

### Measuring Impact and the Event Store [28:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U2p01LVC5Oo&t=1717s)
Four years ago, Amos's team built an event system to debug their Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC). This system collects millions of daily events—commits, CI builds, deployments, and ticket metadata—to investigate bottlenecks. This foundation allows them to answer whether AI actually helps deliver more features, separating "keeping the lights on" (KTLO) work from business-as-usual and feature development.

### Goodhart's Law and Investigative Metrics [34:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U2p01LVC5Oo&t=2050s)
Amos warns against using easily gamable metrics like lines of code or commit counts. He references the "Cobra Effect" (Goodhart's Law): when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Instead of static dashboards, Booking.com uses a query-based approach, allowing leaders to ask contextual questions of the data in real time using AI.

### Bridging Technical Data and Business Goals [40:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U2p01LVC5Oo&t=2405s)
The gap between technical event stores and business revenue goals is bridged by having business leaders define what they want to achieve (e.g., modernization, new features). With a reliable data store, teams can now use AI to write SQL queries on the fly, making it easier to have serendipitous, investigative conversations with executives.

### Vibe Coding and the New Builders [53:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U2p01LVC5Oo&t=3234s)
As non-engineers begin "vibe coding" their own solutions, they will need help with maintenance, observability, and compliance—concepts they aren't used to. Amos compares this to how people previously used Excel as a universal database and UI. Platform teams will need to provide enterprise-grade guardrails for these new builders.

### Token Maxing vs. Effectiveness [76:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U2p01LVC5Oo&t=4580s)
Amos dismisses the concept of "token maxing" (giving engineers strict token budgets). He prefers to push engineers to be as effective as possible, noting that an engineer who achieves great results with fewer tokens is having a better experience. Token budgets might work for initial adoption phases but are not a long-term strategy.

### Developer Experience Equals Velocity [77:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U2p01LVC5Oo&t=4673s)
Amos's team has proven that developer experience and velocity are the same. By creating a seamless, high-trust environment where engineers feel like they are working on side projects, productivity naturally increases. He cites *The Sovereign Individual* as a prophetic book that inspires this focus on empowering the individual.
