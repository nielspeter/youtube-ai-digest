# The Creator of Claude Code Said to Do What Now?!

## TL;DR
Boris Cherny, creator of Claude Code, suggested developers should periodically delete their entire AI layer (rules, skills, hooks) to see what modern models like Opus 5 can do without heavy guidance—but his actual recommendation is a process called "ablation," where you strip things away and add them back only as needed. Cole Medin agrees that pruning over-specified rules is valuable as LLMs improve, but pushes back on wiping everything every six months, arguing it's too token-expensive and impractical; instead, he recommends focusing ablation primarily on global rules, since project-specific conventions still matter even to top-tier models.

## Key Takeaways
- Boris Cherny's "delete everything" comment refers to **ablation**—removing your AI layer and reintroducing components line by line to measure their actual impact, not blindly trusting the model.
- As LLMs improve, overly specific step-by-step instructions can **hurt** performance; you should describe tasks, guardrails, and exit criteria, then "let the model cook."
- Full ablation across your entire AI layer is **extremely token-heavy and expensive**, making it impractical for most developers who don't have unlimited budgets.
- The AI layer exists on a spectrum: **global rules** (loaded every conversation) need aggressive pruning, while **skills, sub-agents, and on-demand context** rarely need ablation.
- Anthropic's own documentation recommends keeping `claw.md` files **under 200 lines** to avoid important rules getting lost in noise.
- Cole's testing on his Archon codebase showed that a stripped-bare AI layer performed **equally well on architectural decisions** but **failed at project-specific conventions** (test registration style, function formatting, import patterns).
- Rules that **fix LLM reasoning gaps** have decayed in value as models improved, but rules that **direct attention and customize behavior** to your codebase remain essential.
- Cole built and released a **Claude skill** to guide developers through the ablation process, available in a new GitHub skills repo.

## Detailed Breakdown

### The Viral Quote and Its Misinterpretation [00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VnyGs43eiAA&t=0s)
Boris Cherny's statement that every six months you should "delete your entire AI layer, your global rules, your skills, your hooks" sparked concern that developers' hard work was being deemed obsolete by Opus 5. Cole clarifies that taking the quote at face value misses important nuance—Boris isn't saying your work is worthless, and the full context reveals a more measured process.

### Ablation, Not Deletion [02:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VnyGs43eiAA&t=124s)
Playing a second clip from the Y Combinator event, Cole reveals what Boris actually described: **ablation**. The Anthropic team doesn't delete the entire codebase—they delete the system prompt and bring it back line by line to evaluate each instruction's impact. This is an evaluation method, not a directive to blindly trust the model with zero guidance.

### Over-Specification Hurts Modern Models [03:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VnyGs43eiAA&t=187s)
Boris identifies a common mistake: giving modern models overly specific step-by-step instructions ("do this, then this, then this"). For current-generation LLMs, the better approach is to describe the task, guardrails, and exit criteria at a higher level, then let the model work autonomously. Ablation helps identify where you've been too specific.

### The Token Cost Problem [04:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VnyGs43eiAA&t=281s)
Cole's main disagreement with Boris is practicality. Full ablation is extremely token-heavy and expensive. He observes that Boris, working at Anthropic with presumably unlimited token access, seems "blind to the fact that tokens are actually pretty expensive" for enterprise users or subscribers hitting rate limits. Boris casually mentions running an agent for over two weeks to migrate a codebase from Electron to Swift—something most developers simply can't do.

### The Spectrum of AI Layer Components [08:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VnyGs43eiAA&t=531s)
Cole breaks the AI layer into three components: **rules** (global rules and context), **skills/workflows**, and **sub-agents**. He places these on a spectrum of context efficiency. Global rules are loaded at the start of every conversation, creating the highest risk of over-specification and restricting the LLM. Skills and sub-agents are on-demand context, making them far less critical to prune. Anthropic's docs reinforce this, recommending global `claw.md` files stay under 200 lines.

### Where to Agree and Disagree with Boris [10:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VnyGs43eiAA&t=622s)
Cole agrees that global rules need frequent pruning pressure and that testing a shrunken version is worthwhile. However, he disagrees with applying ablation to skills, sub-agents, and other on-demand context every six months. Those components don't constantly consume the agent's "mental bandwidth," so the cost-benefit of ablation doesn't justify the time and tokens. He suggests maybe ablating skills or sub-agents once a year at most.

### Real-World Testing on Archon [12:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VnyGs43eiAA&t=775s)
Cole tested ablation on his open-source project Archon, which has a 1,000-line global rule file (well over Anthropic's 200-line recommendation). He stripped it down to roughly 20 lines and removed a 350-line skill entirely, then ran the same GitHub issues side by side with both configurations. The results: the stripped layer performed **equally well on hard architectural decisions** but **failed at project-specific conventions** like test registration patterns, function writing style, and import conventions.

### What Rules Decay vs. What Rules Persist [16:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VnyGs43eiAA&t=960s)
The testing revealed a clear distinction. Rules that **fix reasoning gaps** in LLMs have decayed in value—modern models don't need you to teach them general software engineering. But rules that **direct attention and customize behavior** to your specific codebase remain critical. You can't fully ablate your AI layer because your agent still needs to learn how to meet your specific standards, not just produce generally good code.

### Final Recommendations and the Ablation Skill [17:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VnyGs43eiAA&t=1024s)
Cole recommends starting ablation with your global rules, where low-hanging fruit is easiest to find. Remove rules teaching general practices that fill LLM gaps; keep rules that customize behavior for your codebase. He built and released a Claude skill that automates guidance through the ablation process—identifying your AI layer components, creating test tasks, running them side by side with and without the full layer, and grading the results. It's available in a new GitHub skills repo linked in the description.
