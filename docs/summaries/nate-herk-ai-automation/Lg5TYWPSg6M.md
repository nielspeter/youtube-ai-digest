# 18 Months of Pricing AI Automations in 21 Mins

## TL;DR
Nate Herk shares 18 months of lessons from selling over 100 AI automation systems, arguing that value-based pricing—anchored to the client's own cost-of-problem numbers—beats hourly billing. He walks through a real appointment-setting build, shows how to derive a defensible price as a fraction of annualized savings, and explains how to structure milestones, handle objections, and manage API costs so you never carry more than ~30 days of unpaid work.

## Key Takeaways
- **Price off value, not cost or hours.** Your cost is the floor; the client's value (what the problem costs them) is the ceiling; price is anywhere in between. "Cost doesn't justify price—price justifies cost."
- **Get the client to state the cost of the problem in their own words before you name a number.** Your price then becomes a fraction of a number they've already acknowledged.
- **Aim for a 10x ROI for the client in year one.** In the example, a $5,500 build against $41,600 in annualized labor savings (~7.5x) was justified with projected volume growth to reach 10x.
- **Charge a simple, standard maintenance retainer** (e.g., $400/month) that covers keeping the system working—not new features.
- **Don't bill hourly beyond your first 2–3 projects.** Hourly punishes speed and rewards slowness; as AI tooling makes you faster, hourly cuts your income.
- **Use tiered proposals (starter/growth/scale).** Three options shift the client's question from "should we hire you?" to "which package should we pick?"
- **Set objective, provable milestones** so payment can't be argued with—e.g., "agent pulls from the database and responds within a minute."
- **Handle scope creep by parking new ideas in a "version two" backlog** rather than saying no outright.
- **If the price is too high, reduce scope—not price.** This protects your value and avoids training the client that frowning lowers your rate.
- **API and token costs go on the client's own account/card.** You cover testing costs during build (factored into your price), then swap in the client's keys for production.

## Detailed Breakdown

### The Appointment-Setting Example and Value Math [00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Lg5TYWPSg6M&t=0s)
Nate opens with a real build: a business had employees manually setting ~20 meetings/week, each taking an hour at $40/hour all-in. That's $800/week or ~$41,600/year. He priced the build at ~13% of that annualized value—$5,500—yielding roughly a 7.5x return. He notes the "golden rule" is showing a 10x return, and the gap to 10x was covered by projecting that freed-up capacity would let the business handle more leads over time. A standard $400/month maintenance retainer was added, covering only bug fixes and keeping the system functional—not new features.

### The Mistake of Not Measuring the After State [04:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Lg5TYWPSg6M&t=244s)
Nate admits he failed to go back and measure the system's actual impact after deployment—no "after state" or transformation number. That absence hurt him in later sales conversations. He stresses capturing the baseline (e.g., 20 leads/week, speed-to-lead time) up front, then following up at 1, 2, and 3 months to prove the numbers moved. If you don't surface the impact, the client may not feel it even when the automation is genuinely helping.

### Why Not Bill Hourly [05:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Lg5TYWPSg6M&t=306s)
Citing Jonathan Stark's talk at AI's Live, Nate explains hourly billing's incentive problem: your slowest developer earns you three times what your fastest one does for the same task. As AI tooling (e.g., cloud-based coding tools) makes you faster, hourly pricing actively cuts your income. He allows hourly for your first 2–3 projects (~$100/hour) when you lack proof, but recommends moving to value-based pricing after that.

### Cost, Value, and Price Framework [06:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Lg5TYWPSg6M&t=399s)
Three numbers to keep straight: **cost** (your floor—walk away if below), **value** (the client's ceiling—what the problem is worth to them), and **price** (any negotiated number in between). On AI builds the gap is enormous because what it costs you to build is far less than what the solution is worth to the business. Nate repeats Jonathan Stark's landscaper analogy: a landscaper can't double your price just because he bought a fancy new truck—cost doesn't justify price.

### Discovery: Finding the Ceiling [08:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Lg5TYWPSg6M&t=494s)
You get one conversation to uncover the client's value ceiling. Nate's approach: let them dump everything ("tell me everything"), then run the **LRP framework**—listen, repeat, poke. Pivot to "what changes once this is successfully running?" Then ask three buckets: **Why this?** (does what they asked for actually produce the outcome?), **Why now?** (urgency—competitor pressure, closing windows), and **Why me?** (why not do it internally, hand it to an intern, or vibe-code it?). The "why me" question surfaces objections early so you can handle them on the call rather than losing the deal later.

### When Clients Won't Give You Numbers [10:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Lg5TYWPSg6M&t=647s)
Many clients won't share salaries or bottom-line figures. Start with three sizing questions: *How long does this take today? How many people touch it? What happens when it goes wrong?* Stack proxy questions (number of locations, busiest-day volume, etc.). On job marketplaces where you must submit a price before talking, use the job post to estimate volume/headcount, state your assumption out loud in the proposal, and invite a 15-minute call to adjust—turning a cold price into a conversation starter. If you can't land on a number, you're not ready to write the proposal.

### Deriving the Price and Tiered Proposals [11:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Lg5TYWPSg6M&t=710s)
Take the first-year annualized value and price at 10–20% of it. Always assume the client will ask, "How did you get that number?" and answer confidently with their own numbers. In proposals, lead with three short paragraphs: where the business is now (their words, their numbers), where they want to be, and why you're the person to get them there. Then present **tiered packages**—e.g., 10% ($10k), 25% ($25k), 50% ($50k) of a $100k value. The middle tier is designed to win; the bottom looks thin, the top makes them wince, and the middle looks correct. Psychologically, three options change the question from "should we hire you?" to "how should we work with you?"

### Thinking Beyond Hours Saved to Bottom-Line Impact [13:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Lg5TYWPSg6M&t=804s)
Nate shares a simple but highly profitable automation: converting a construction crew's phone orders into their existing text format, saving 45 minutes/day but avoiding ~$12,000/month in scheduling errors. That's hard dollars lost, not just freed-up hours. He encourages, as you gain experience, to value systems on bottom-line impact—e.g., for the appointment setter, what if each closed sale was worth $5,000? That reframing could have justified a much higher price.

### Underscoping and Objective Milestones [15:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Lg5TYWPSg6M&t=900s)
Underscoping was Nate's #1 early mistake, causing timeline disputes and milestone arguments. He structures projects with milestones ~30 days apart, each tied to a payment. The key: milestones must be **objective and provable**, not subjective. A good example: "There's a POC in the owner's hands; when they ask a question, the agent pulls from the database and responds within a minute." A bad example: "the inbox agent is working as expected"—too ambiguous, invites scope creep and delayed payment.

### Handling Scope Creep [17:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Lg5TYWPSg6M&t=1022s)
When clients try to add functionality mid-project, Nate doesn't say no. He says, "Great idea—let's put that on the backlog for version two." This keeps momentum on current milestones while capturing their excitement for future work. You keep adding ideas to the backlog, reinforcing that hitting existing milestones quickly delivers the most value.

### Responding to Price Pushback [17:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Lg5TYWPSg6M&t=1052s)
If a client says the price is beyond their budget, Nate suggests reducing scope rather than lowering the price: "Okay, $20k isn't in the budget right now—let's start with a smaller piece, and once it's winning back time and business, we'll move to the next phase." This avoids training the client that your price drops every time they frown, and it keeps the work fairly valued.

### API Costs, Testing Costs, and Production Handoff [18:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Lg5TYWPSg6M&t=1083s)
Nate's rule: **client's account, client's card, every time.** Your fee covers design, consulting, building, and testing; token/API costs are a utility bill that belongs in the client's name. He used to run everything under his own billing and invoice monthly—it got messy, required babysitting, and eroded trust. In proposals, include an estimated monthly run cost with the volume assumption noted. During testing, Nate pays for everything (factored into the price by bumping it $1,000–$3,000+ depending on testing rigor), then swaps in the client's API keys when moving to production—so the partnership doesn't start by spending the client's money before showing any value.

### The One Thing to Take Into Your Next Call [20:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Lg5TYWPSg6M&t=1238s)
Before naming any number, get the client to articulate—out loud, in their own words—what the problem is costing them. Once that figure is on the table, your price is simply a fraction of a number they've already stated. "What you're selling is the result, and the bill is just how it gets delivered."
