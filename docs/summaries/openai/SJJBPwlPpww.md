## TL;DR
A research collaboration between the Manton Center for Orphan Disease Research at Boston Children's Hospital and OpenAI used AI-driven workflows to help diagnose rare genetic diseases. By applying OpenAI's o3 deep research model to 376 unsolved "cold cases," the workflow surfaced evidence that led to 18 new rare disease diagnoses, dramatically reducing the time experts spend searching through massive genomic datasets.

## Key Takeaways
* The average time to diagnose a rare disease is six to seven years, a grueling process often referred to as the "diagnostic odyssey."
* The human genome contains roughly three billion bases and 20,000 genes, with 8,000 to 9,000 known to be associated with disease; analyzing the millions of resulting data variants manually is a massive bottleneck.
* AI models like OpenAI's o3 deep research can rapidly process curated genetic variants and patient symptom codes to nominate a short, evidence-backed list of potential disease-causing genes.
* In a study of 376 previously unsolved cases, the AI workflow helped researchers identify 18 new diagnoses.
* The AI does not replace human diagnosticians; it narrows the search space so experts can focus their time and attention on validating the most promising leads.
* Getting a correct diagnosis unlocks critical benefits for patients: community, informed family planning, prognosis clarity, and access to targeted drug therapies and clinical trials.
* The team envisions a future where agentic AI approaches can periodically and inexpensively re-run old cases as new medical literature and gene-disease associations are published.
* Whole genome sequencing is becoming faster and cheaper (under $1,000), and should ideally be performed early in a patient's diagnostic journey.

### The Patient Journey and Diagnostic Odyssey [01:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SJJBPwlPpww&t=103s)
Stav Rones, a software engineer based in Miami, shared his personal story of living with a rare neuromuscular disease. He first noticed physical limitations and pain as a competitive teenager playing sports. It took three to four years just to figure out his symptoms were neurological, and another five years to confirm the issue was genetic. Getting his genome sequenced and finally receiving a correct diagnosis brought immense clarity. It allowed him to join a patient community, make informed family planning choices using modern reproductive technology, and connect with a biotech company developing a drug specifically for his condition.

### Why Rare Diseases Are Hard to Solve [05:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SJJBPwlPpww&t=352s)
Dr. Alan Begs explained that the human genome has three billion bases encoding about 20,000 genes. While next-generation sequencing allows researchers to sequence everything, it leaves them with massive datasets containing hundreds of thousands to millions of genetic variants. Dr. Catherine Brownstein added that analysts must manually filter these down—looking at de novo mutations, inherited variants, and deletions—then cross-reference databases and read extensive literature for each candidate gene. This exhaustive manual process means many cases remain unsolved for years.

### The Impact of a Diagnosis [11:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SJJBPwlPpww&t=665s)
Begs noted that for many rare diseases, like neuromuscular conditions, medical management is largely supportive (e.g., orthopedic surgeries). Identifying the exact genetic cause opens the door to personalized medicine and targeted therapies. Brownstein shared a story about diagnosing a 91-year-old patient whose family had been affected across three generations, highlighting the profound emotional relief families experience when they finally get a name for their condition.

### The AI Workflow and Model Iteration [16:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SJJBPwlPpww&t=980s)
Suya Shringerpur explained that the team used OpenAI's o3 deep research model to accelerate the literature search and hypothesis generation. The model was fed curated genetic data (variants filtered down to a manageable size) and numerical ontological codes representing the patient's clinical phenotype. The AI was prompted to intersect these data points and produce an evidence-driven list of suspected genes. The team first tested the model on known, solved cases, iterating on the prompts to help the AI avoid common technical pitfalls (like false positives from low sequencing depth). Once the model reached 80-90% accuracy on solved cases, they applied it to unsolved ones.

### Cracking the Cold Cases [23:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SJJBPwlPpww&t=1418s)
Out of 376 unsolved cases, the AI workflow surfaced 18 new diagnoses. Brownstein shared a compelling example (Case 151) involving a patient with vitiligo, transposition of the great vessels, and pulmonary hypertension. The AI nominated a gene called S1PR1. Brownstein searched the literature and found a 26-year-old paper supporting the AI's hypothesis. In a stroke of luck, the researcher who originally cloned S1PR1 in 1988 worked in the building next door at Boston Children's Hospital, allowing them to immediately collaborate on the finding. The AI was able to do deep literature searches (going "to page 30" of PubMed results) that humans simply do not have the time to perform.

### The Future of AI in Medicine [29:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SJJBPwlPpww&t=1760s)
The panelists shared their hopes for AI in healthcare. Shringerpur envisions AI re-analyzing cases automatically as new papers are published, freeing researchers to focus on the hardest 95% of unsolved cases. Begs noted that agentic AI could review data in the background and flag new findings, emphasizing that AI will make diagnosticians faster, not replace them. Brownstein hopes for a world where an undiagnosed case is run through AI, matched with a diagnosis, and connected to the right clinical trial. The team is currently working on a tool, supported by a grant from the OpenAI Foundation, to make this technology secure and accessible to clinicians worldwide.

### Community Q&A [33:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SJJBPwlPpww&t=1980s)
During the audience Q&A, Begs stressed that whole genome sequencing should be done early in a diagnostic journey, noting it now costs under $1,000 and can sometimes yield diagnoses within 24 hours. Brownstein answered a question about AI connecting unrelated health aspects, recalling a test case where the AI correctly identified that a patient had two separate genetic issues occurring simultaneously, a complexity the human reviewers had initially missed. Shringerpur noted that while curated data integration was key to their current success, the future vision is to feed the model a patient's full medical record and entire genome file without needing manual curation.
