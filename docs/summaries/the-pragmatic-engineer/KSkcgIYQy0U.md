## TL;DR
Hillel Wayne, formal methods consultant and author of *Logic for Programmers*, explains how formal specification languages like TLA+ and Alloy help engineers find bugs in distributed systems by making implicit knowledge explicit and brute-forcing state spaces. He also explores the "Crossover Project" comparing software engineering to traditional engineering, the practical value of property-based testing as a middle ground, and why AI is unlikely to make formal verification go mainstream—though it can help experts work faster.

## Key Takeaways
- **Formal methods** make implicit knowledge about what a function should do explicit, then use mathematical proof or model checking to verify the implementation matches the specification for *all* possible inputs—not just tested ones.
- **TLA+**, invented by Leslie Lamport, models systems as state machines and uses brute-force model checking to explore every possible state transition. AWS used it to find a 35-step bug in DynamoDB replication.
- **Formal methods are not for everything.** Most real-world domain problems pull in too much context (file systems, permissions, edge cases) to make full verification practical. They shine for discrete distributed systems, databases, and cryptographic primitives.
- **Property-based testing** is a practical middle ground: instead of proving correctness for all inputs, it generates thousands of random inputs and checks properties, then "shrinks" failures to minimal examples.
- **The Crossover Project** found that software engineers *are* real engineers. Across 15+ interviews spanning 6-7 engineering fields, the core similarity was a tension between iteration speed and mistake cost. Everyone hates waterfall.
- **Software engineering is uniquely privileged** in open source, practitioner conferences, and version control—all 20 engineers Hillel interviewed from other fields envied Git-style version control.
- **Concurrency bugs are hard** not because humans can't reason about concurrency, but because we rarely get practice. TLA+ compresses the feedback loop from months to seconds.
- **AI is bad at generating properties** for formal specifications. It can fix syntax, explain error traces, and write boilerplate, but it struggles to express what a system is supposed to do—especially liveness properties.
- **Discrete math** (combinatorics, graph theory, set theory, formal logic) is the math software engineers actually need, but it's rarely taught in high school or early university, unlike the continuous math traditional engineers use.
- Hillel fears software engineering may lose its "magical" privileges—high salaries, bargaining power, and creative freedom—as AI normalizes the field into ordinary white-collar work.

## Detailed Breakdown

### The Crossover Project: Are Software Engineers Real Engineers? [04:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KSkcgIYQy0U&t=279s)

Hillel initially believed software developers were *not* real engineers and didn't deserve the title. After finding a talk by Glenn Vanderberg arguing the opposite, he decided to interview 15-20 people across 6-7 traditional engineering fields (mining, chemical, electrical, civil, industrial, and more) to settle the question rigorously. He discovered they all agreed with Vanderberg: software engineering *is* engineering.

The core finding was that every engineering discipline shares a fundamental tension between **how expensive it is to make a mistake** and **how quickly you can iterate**. Civil engineers build scale models and run CAD simulations because they can't rebuild a skyscraper. Chemical engineers run overnight experiments. Software engineers press F11. Every field tries to iterate as fast as possible, but the cost of errors dictates how much upfront planning is required. All fields hate waterfall.

One surprising difference: software is far more **consistent** than physical engineering. A CPU spec sheet might say a resistor is within 20% of 100 ohms—physical materials have variance. Software, barring CPU bugs, runs identically everywhere. Hillel also found that **open source** and **practitioner conferences** are unique to software. Other fields have academic conferences and trade shows, but not communities of practitioners sharing knowledge freely. He speculates this stems from software being both the tool and the product—the same material on both ends.

Version control was the single most envied tool. All 20 interviewees mentioned it as something they wished their fields had.

### What Formal Methods Are and Why They're Not Used Everywhere [18:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KSkcgIYQy0U&t=1098s)

Hillel introduces formal methods with a simple example: the `max` function. When you look at `max(2, 3)` and know the answer is 3, you're using implicit knowledge. Formal methods step one is making that explicit: "the max of a list is an element in the list such that every other element is smaller." Step two is verification—showing the implementation satisfies that specification for *every possible input*, not just tested cases, using mathematical proof.

But when the host suggests verifying a function that finds the file with the most lines in a directory, Hillel quickly demonstrates the problem: you immediately hit questions about file permissions, symlinks, directories, encoding, and more. Writing the specification becomes a nightmare because real-world domain problems pull in enormous context. The imperative program that works 99% of the time is good enough, and writing 10 tests gets you most of the value for a fraction of the effort.

### Where Formal Methods Are Actually Used in Industry [24:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KSkcgIYQy0U&t=1488s)

Hillel categorizes formal methods usage:

1. **Nukes and NASA**—Hillel notes nuclear power plants actually don't care much; thorough testing suffices for them.
2. **Critical cores of programs**—small, verified parts of larger systems: cryptographic primitives (Firefox's HTTPS stack via Project Everest), parts of OS kernels (Microsoft verified driver loading in the Vista kernel; the seL4 microkernel was end-to-end verified in Isabelle).
3. **Abstract models of systems**—Hillel's main work. You create a simplified model, verify that, and iron out design bugs before building the real system. His clients are mostly database vendors, cloud computing companies, and hardware firms. He's also verified train transponder firmware and found an old bug there.

### TLA+ Demo: A Trading Platform Bug [29:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KSkcgIYQy0U&t=1770s)

Hillel demonstrates TLA+, invented by Leslie Lamport (also creator of LaTeX) in 1994. TLA+ represents a system as a state machine—every possible state and transition—and uses brute-force model checking to explore all of them.

The demo models a simple trading platform: Alice, Bob, and Carol trade a stick. Alice offers the stick to Bob. Bob is away. Alice offers to Carol. Carol accepts—stick transfers to Carol. Bob returns, accepts the original offer, and the stick transfers to Carol's stick to Bob. The "change invariant" (if the stick goes from X to Y, it must be because X offered and Y accepted) is violated because the stick went from Carol to Bob based on Alice's offer.

The model checker found this in 53 states. For real systems, state spaces can reach 100 million states, churned overnight. Hillel emphasizes this is where TLA+ shines for distributed systems: humans can't mentally enumerate every interleaving of concurrent processes, but a computer can brute-force them.

### AWS and TLA+ [36:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KSkcgIYQy0U&t=2209s)

The seminal 2014 paper "Use of Formal Methods at Amazon Web Services" described how a few interested engineers learned TLA+ and PlusCal (which compiles to TLA+) and applied them to DynamoDB and S3. They found complicated bugs in the replication system that could potentially lose data. The shortest error trace exhibiting one bug contained **35 high-level steps**—a depth very difficult for a human to find or persevere through manually. Hillel speculates the state space was around 100 million states, with many safe 70-80 step chains and one invalid 35-step chain.

### Common Distributed System Bugs and Why Concurrency Is Hard [38:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KSkcgIYQy0U&t=2301s)

Hillel identifies **time-of-check-to-time-of-use (TOCTOU)** bugs as a recurring pattern: you check something is valid, then use it later, but it changed in between. The bank withdrawal example—checking $10 is available, then someone else grabs it, then you deduct and get negative $10—illustrates this.

The host notes that exactly-once delivery in distributed systems is notoriously hard (easier to do at-least-once). Hillel adds that hotels authorize large credit holds upfront precisely to avoid TOCTOU edge cases where a customer's credit runs out.

On why concurrency is hard: Hillel references a paper called "Common Sense Computing" where researchers found people solved concurrency bugs much faster when framed as real-world scenarios (clerks assigning concert seats) rather than abstract thread operations. He argues the real problem is **lack of practice**—in production, you discover a race condition months later, try a fix, and wait weeks to see if it worked. With TLA+, you click a button and immediately see the race condition, fix it, see the next bug, and so on. The compressed feedback loop builds expertise.

### Math for Programmers [46:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KSkcgIYQy0U&t=2768s)

Hillel distinguishes between math everyone uses without thinking (counting, comparison), math for specialists (calculus for some robotics), and math broadly useful in programming (graphs, matrices, formal logic). He argues most developers should have *exposure* to what's available rather than deep mastery of every field.

A key insight: traditional engineering uses **continuous math** (differential equations, calculus), which is what's taught in US high schools. Software engineering needs **discrete math** (combinatorics, graph theory, set theory, formal logic), which is rarely taught early. This mismatch may explain why people don't recognize math's relevance to software.

### When TLA+ Is and Isn't a Good Fit [50:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KSkcgIYQy0U&t=3016s)

TLA+ shines for **highly computational, technical domains** (database replication, distributed systems) rather than business-embedded problems (sprint planning, human behavior). Hillel's clients are typically database vendors, cloud computing companies, and hardware firms—important to business but several steps removed from the front lines.

TLA+ specifically is good for **discrete distributed systems** with concurrency and liveness concerns. It doesn't handle floating point, decimals, or probabilistic reasoning. It's the wrong tool if bugs are tolerable ("as long as it happens less than 1 in 100 times, it's fine") or if iteration is cheap and bugs aren't costly. Hillel stresses he doesn't want to push formal methods where they don't fit, comparing overzealous advocacy to CASE tools and UML.

### Alloy Demo: Access Control Bug [52:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KSkcgIYQy0U&t=3171s)

Hillel shows Alloy, made by an MIT professor, which uses SAT solving rather than brute-force model checking. The demo models an access control system: resources have parents, users can read resources if assigned or if a parent is assigned, and the property states that if you can read a resource, you can read its children.

The bug: `readable_by` is not transitive. You can read a parent, therefore you can read its child, but you *cannot* read the child's child (grandchild) because you're only assigned to the parent, not the child. Alloy generates a visual counterexample showing the gap.

Alloy converts models into boolean satisfiability problems and solves them in milliseconds to seconds, versus TLA+ which may need overnight runs. However, Alloy is worse for modeling distributed systems with temporal behavior.

### Other Formal Methods Tools [58:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KSkcgIYQy0U&t=3516s)

Hillel surveys the landscape:
- **P language**—invented at Microsoft Research, poached by Amazon; looks like interacting state machines (actor model).
- **Quint**—easier-to-learn alternative to TLA+; popular in banking and cryptocurrency/smart contracts.
- **Prism**—probabilistic model checker; can tell you a bug happens 10% of the time. More academic; Hillel used it to analyze the dreidel game.
- **Event-B**—used in the Paris Metro system.
- **mCRL2**—from a Dutch university.
- **NuSMV**—used by NASA.
- For code-level verification: **Dafny**, **JML** (Java), **Prusti** (Rust), **Frama-C** (C), **Ada/SPARK**, and theorem provers like **Rocq**, **Lean**, **Isabelle**.

### Property-Based Testing as the Practical Middle Ground [61:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KSkcgIYQy0U&t=3671s)

Hillel demonstrates property-based testing using Python and Hypothesis. Instead of writing 5-8 hand-crafted tests, you define properties (e.g., "the max of a list is in the list and all other elements are smaller") and the tool generates thousands of random lists to check. When it finds a failure, it **shrinks** the input to the minimally interesting example—turning a huge random list into something like `[0, 0, 0, 0, 1]` that a human can immediately understand.

Hillel considers property-based testing more broadly useful than formal methods for most engineers. It's less thorough but far easier to apply.

### AI and Formal Verification [65:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KSkcgIYQy0U&t=3953s)

Hillel is seeing more business from clients trying to generate specs with AI. AI is good at:
- Fixing syntax errors
- Explaining error traces (turning 35-step traces into English paragraphs)
- Writing boilerplate
- Translating precise English descriptions into specification properties

AI is **bad at**:
- Coming up with properties on its own (it generates trivially true properties like "either P is true or not P is true")
- Fixing specs
- Expressing liveness properties

A blog post from March 2025 described AI autonomously producing TLA+ specs from Azure storage code and finding a subtle race condition. Hillel notes the author, Chang Huang, was already an expert specifier, and one system he demonstrated already had a sophisticated spec in P—so it's unclear how much the AI "cheated." Hillel sees AI as a **specification force multiplier** for existing experts, not a path to mainstream adoption. He estimates it's moving formal methods from ~0.1% to ~0.3% adoption—huge in relative terms, but still niche.

### Hillel's Anxieties About the Future of Software Engineering [74:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KSkcgIYQy0U&t=4481s)

A year ago, Hillel wrote six predictions:
1. Vibe coders will never be as good as experienced software engineers—probably still true.
2. LLMs significantly augment experienced engineers—true.
3. LLMs will cause job losses—unclear; recent hiring recovery may be more about post-ZIRP correction than AI.
4. LLMs will open new jobs—true, but you hire one developer instead of five.
5. New jobs will be lower-paid—Hillel's biggest fear. He treasures software engineering's unique privileges (high pay, bargaining power, creative freedom, travel) and fears it becoming "any other white collar job."
6. High-paid jobs will remain but be rarer and more competitive—he's seeing signs of this already.

He closes with a story about a doctor friend who vibe-coded a shift scheduling platform that made nurses and doctors happier, illustrating the tension between his professional anxiety and the democratizing benefits of AI.

1. **Engineering a Safer World** by Nancy Leveson—free online; an aeronautics engineer's investigation of how complex systems fail (Challenger, radiation cases). Hillel's top recommendation for engineers building complicated systems.
